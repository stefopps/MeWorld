Alpha, discovered not defined

Before the curve, the bouncer

Forget the bell curve for a second. Here are 100 separate, independent trials — in every single one, the drug does absolutely nothing. The null hypothesis is actually, truly true. Drag the line. Find out how strict a bouncer you want to be.

Think of the threshold like a bouncer at a club checking IDs. A strict bouncer (line far to the right) almost never lets a fake ID through — but turns away more real regulars by mistake too. A loose bouncer (line closer to the middle) lets more real people in, but more fakes slip past. Every one of the 100 dots below is a "regular" — nothing fake about any of them. Any dot that gets flagged by your line is a false alarm, full stop.

Drag the line until exactly 5 dots get flagged. That's the bouncer setting the whole field calls "standard."
You just found it yourself: α = 0.05 — a 5% false-alarm rate — isn't a rule handed down from anywhere. It's just "how strict does the line need to be so that, out of 100 truly-null trials, about 5 slip past by chance." Drag further right (stricter) and re-roll a few times — notice the flagged count won't always land on exactly 5 even at the same line position. That's sampling variability, the same reason two trials with the identical true effect can still land on different sides of "significant."
Now zoom out
The smooth bell curve you've seen everywhere else isn't a different idea from these dots — it's what this exact same picture looks like once you stop being able to count individual trials. The shaded region under the curve is just this dot-counting exercise, run so many times it blurs into a shape.
Pattern demonstrated here: discrete, countable trials before continuous abstraction; discovery of the threshold before the formal name is attached to it; a concrete metaphor (bouncer) with zero Greek letters before any notation appears. This is a companion to the curve-based power demo — the two are meant to be encountered in this order, dots first.
