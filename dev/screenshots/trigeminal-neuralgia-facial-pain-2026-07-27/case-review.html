First Principles · The Mechanism
The trigeminal nerve exits the pons and crosses the prepontine cistern. Right there, the superior cerebellar artery loops over the nerve root entry zone. In 80-90% of cases, this artery compresses the nerve. The compression strips away myelin. Demyelinated axons fire ectopically and form ephaptic connections — a touch signal on one axon jumps across to a pain fiber on its neighbor. A breeze on the cheek becomes lightning in the jaw. Carbamazepine blocks sodium channels, raising the threshold for firing in those demyelinated axons. It doesn't fix the compression — it just makes the nerve less trigger-happy.

What I Missed 3 diagnostic gaps
▾
CBC with differential
Facial pain in a patient with recent dental procedures — you must rule out acute infection before calling it neuralgic. A leukocytosis or bandemia would change the entire workup from neuro to infectious.
Comprehensive metabolic profile
Carbamazepine causes hyponatremia via SIADH. You need a pre-treatment sodium level before starting therapy. If sodium is 134 and drops to 125 after starting carbamazepine, you caught it. If you never checked baseline, you won't know whether the drop is drug-induced or pre-existing.
CT/MRI head (with or without contrast)
80-90% of trigeminal neuralgia is caused by a vascular loop compressing the nerve root. But the remaining 10-20% are structural lesions — tumor, MS plaque, or aneurysm. You image to confirm the diagnosis and rule out the things that would change management. A cerebellopontine angle tumor presenting as trigeminal neuralgia is rare, but catastrophic to miss.
The Attending's Lesson
Trigeminal neuralgia is a clinical diagnosis — paroxysmal, electric, seconds-to-minutes, triggered by innocuous stimuli, respecting trigeminal distribution. The exam is almost always normal between attacks. The treatment is carbamazepine. The imaging is to rule out a structural mimic, not to confirm what you already know. But the CBC and CMP are not optional — infection mimics neuralgia, and carbamazepine demands a pre-treatment sodium.

If I Were Your Attending Sequential walkthrough
▾
1
General appearance + Neuro exam
You need to see the patient. Is he in acute distress during the attack? Is the neuro exam normal between paroxysms? Trigeminal neuralgia has a normal exam between attacks — if there are sensory deficits or motor weakness, think tumor or MS, not idiopathic TN.
2
CBC with differential
He had multiple dental procedures. A periapical abscess or osteomyelitis of the jaw can present as facial pain mimicking TN. A CBC rules out acute infection before you commit to a neurologic diagnosis.
3
Comprehensive metabolic profile
Carbamazepine is going to be prescribed today. I need a baseline sodium, creatinine, and LFTs before therapy starts. CBZ induces SIADH and can elevate transaminases. I don't start without knowing where we began.
4
MRI head with contrast
High-resolution T2-weighted imaging of the posterior fossa. I'm looking at the trigeminal nerve root entry zone. Is there a vascular loop? Good — 80-90% of cases are neurovascular compression, which responds to carbamazepine. Is there a cerebellopontine angle tumor or an MS plaque? That changes everything. I image to rule out the catastrophic, not to confirm the obvious.
5
Carbamazepine 200 mg BID
Start low, titrate up. The goal is pain freedom within 1-2 weeks. I check sodium and LFTs at 2 weeks and 1 month. If it fails, I try oxcarbazepine. If that fails, I escalate to gabapentin or baclofen. Surgery (microvascular decompression) is reserved for medically refractory cases — and this patient's not there yet.
