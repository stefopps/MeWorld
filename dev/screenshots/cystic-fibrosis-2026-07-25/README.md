## Mechanism: The CFTR Protein — A Chloride Channel That Governs Every Wet Surface

The cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator — CFTR — is a cAMP-regulated chloride channel on the apical membrane of epithelial cells. It sits on the surface of cells lining the airways, pancreatic ducts, sweat ducts, bile ducts, vas deferens, and intestinal epithelium. When it opens, chloride ions flow out of the cell into the lumen. Water follows chloride osmotically.

In CF, the CFTR gene on chromosome 7 has a mutation — most commonly delta-F508, a three-base-pair deletion that removes phenylalanine at position 508. This misfolds the protein. The quality control machinery in the endoplasmic reticulum recognizes the misfolded protein and targets it for degradation. It never reaches the cell surface.

The result: chloride cannot exit the cell. Water cannot follow. The secretions on every epithelial surface become thick, dehydrated, and sticky.

**In the lungs:** The airway surface liquid layer thins. Mucociliary clearance fails — the tiny cilia that sweep mucus up and out of the airways beat in a dehydrated film and cannot move the thickened mucus. Bacteria colonize the stagnant mucus. First Staphylococcus aureus, then Haemophilus influenzae, then — almost inevitably — Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Pseudomonas forms biofilms, converts to a mucoid phenotype, and becomes impossible to eradicate. The cycle is: mucus stasis → bacterial colonization → neutrophilic inflammation → neutrophil elastase destroys lung architecture → bronchiectasis → more mucus stasis. This child's CXR showed unilateral hyperinflation and pneumonia. That is the beginning of the end if the cycle is not broken.

**In the pancreas:** The pancreatic ducts are tiny. Even a modest increase in secretion viscosity plugs them. Digestive enzymes — lipase, amylase, proteases — are produced normally by the acinar cells, but they cannot reach the duodenum. They autodigest the pancreas instead, causing fibrosis and eventual fatty replacement. The result: pancreatic insufficiency. Fats pass through undigested — the oily stools (steatorrhea) and the failure to thrive (calories lost in stool). Fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K are never absorbed.

**In the sweat glands:** The CFTR defect works in the OPPOSITE direction here. Normally, CFTR in the sweat duct reabsorbs chloride from the lumen back into the cell. In CF, chloride stays in the lumen. Sodium follows. The result: sweat chloride is elevated — >60 mmol/L is diagnostic. This is the basis of the sweat chloride test, the gold standard for CF diagnosis since 1959.

One mutation, one broken channel, three organ systems — lungs, pancreas, sweat glands — each failing in a different way because the same channel does different jobs in different epithelia. That is the spatial elegance of CF pathophysiology.

## Contrast: Why Pseudomonas, Not Strep?

Community-acquired pneumonia in a 3-year-old is usually viral — RSV, influenza, parainfluenza. Bacterial causes are Strep pneumoniae, H. influenzae, Moraxella. Not Pseudomonas.

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a water-loving organism. It thrives in moist environments — hot tubs, respiratory equipment, chronically wet airways. The CF airway is its perfect habitat: stagnant, dehydrated mucus that cannot be cleared. Pseudomonas swims into the mucus plug, attaches to the epithelial surface, and secretes alginate — a polysaccharide biofilm that shields it from antibiotics and the immune system.

This is why the strain in this case was resistant to ceftazidime, gentamicin, tobramycin, amikacin, and ciprofloxacin. Chronic Pseudomonas in CF airways evolves multidrug resistance under constant antibiotic pressure. Antipseudomonal penicillins (piperacillin-tazobactam) and carbapenems (meropenem) remain effective against many strains. You chose correctly.

But the deeper point: Pseudomonas in a 3-year-old who has never been hospitalized is a red flag. It means the airway has been colonized long enough for Pseudomonas to establish. This child's CF has been active and undiagnosed for years. The pneumonia you treated is the tip of an iceberg.

## Connecting Thread: Every Missed Order Tells the Same Story

You knew the diagnosis. You treated the acute infection correctly. But CF is a chronic disease — the missed orders are all about the chronic management that starts on day one of diagnosis.

**Pancreatic enzymes (CREON).** The child is failing to thrive because fat calories are passing through undigested. Pancrelipase replaces what his plugged pancreatic ducts cannot deliver. Given with every meal and snack. Without it, no amount of food will reverse the malnutrition.

**Fat-soluble vitamins (ADEK).** If you cannot absorb fat, you cannot absorb the vitamins dissolved in it. Vitamin A deficiency → night blindness, xerophthalmia. Vitamin D deficiency → rickets, osteopenia. Vitamin E deficiency → hemolytic anemia, neuropathy. Vitamin K deficiency → coagulopathy. Every CF patient gets ADEK from diagnosis onward.

**Chest physiotherapy.** The mucus does not move on its own. Percussion, postural drainage, high-frequency chest wall oscillation — mechanical clearance of the airways is a daily, lifelong requirement. Albuterol before CPT opens the airways so the percussion actually moves mucus rather than just shaking closed bronchi.

**Sweat chloride / CFTR DNA.** You diagnosed CF clinically. Brilliant. But the confirmatory test is not optional — it is how you close the loop, quantify severity, identify the specific mutation (which now matters because CFTR modulators like ivacaftor are mutation-specific), and begin genetic counseling for the family.

**Consults.** CF is not managed by one physician. Nutrition (specialized high-calorie diet, enzyme timing). Genetics (carrier testing for parents, implications for future siblings). Pediatric pulmonology (airway clearance regimen, infection surveillance, pulmonary function tracking as the child grows). CF Foundation-accredited care center (multidisciplinary team, better outcomes than general care).

**Location.** A 3-year-old with pneumonia, hypoxia, newly diagnosed CF, and failure to thrive cannot be managed in the office. Inpatient for IV antibiotics, oxygen, nutritional stabilization, enzyme initiation, and family education.

## Clinical Anchors

- **CF triad in a toddler: pulmonary (SOB, hyperinflation on CXR) + nutritional (weight loss, low albumin) + GI (steatorrhea, oily stools).** This is a pattern-recognition diagnosis. Once you see it, you cannot unsee it.
- **Sweat chloride >60 = CF.** The gold standard diagnostic test. CFTR DNA for mutation identification — now clinically relevant because modulators are mutation-specific.
- **Pseudomonas in a child = CF until proven otherwise.** Community-acquired pneumonia in a 3-year-old is Strep or viral. Pseudomonas means chronic airway colonization.
- **Pancreatic enzymes WITH food, not between meals.** Timing matters. Lipase must be present when fat enters the duodenum.
- **ADEK from day one.** Fat malabsorption = fat-soluble vitamin malabsorption. Every CF patient.
- **Chest physio is not optional.** It is the mechanical equivalent of coughing — and CF patients cannot cough effectively because their mucus is too thick.
- **Admit. Always admit.** New diagnosis of CF with active infection is an inpatient event.
