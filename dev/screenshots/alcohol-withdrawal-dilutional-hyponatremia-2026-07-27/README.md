# Alcohol Withdrawal — Dilutional Hyponatremia, Chronic Alcoholism

**Date:** 2026-07-27 | **Score:** 72.49% (Avg 91.8%) | **Diagnosis:** Alcohol Withdrawal
**Patient:** 52M, chronic alcoholism, fatigue, abdominal distention, memory trouble, lost appetite, last drink 3.3 days ago

---

## Hook

A 52-year-old man walks into your office. He's been drinking heavily for years. He quit 3 days ago. Now he's tachycardic at 108, BP 110/55. His abdomen is distended. His memory is foggy. His liver numbers are through the roof — AST 198, ALT 76, GGT 504. His last drink was 79 hours ago. That puts him at PEAK alcohol withdrawal — the most dangerous window for seizures and DTs.

You gave him chlordiazepoxide, thiamine, folate, and fluids. You diagnosed alcohol withdrawal correctly. You scored 72%.

But you ordered a serum magnesium. You ordered a type and screen. You ordered a serum amylase. You ordered a serum osmolality at the wrong time. Four orders that don't belong in this case. And you may have missed the liver — the ascites, the dilutional hyponatremia, the portal hypertension hiding behind that distended abdomen.

Let me walk you through each one from first principles.

## Mechanism — Why 79 Hours Is the Danger Zone

### The Alcohol Withdrawal Timeline

Alcohol is a CNS depressant. It potentiates GABA-A receptors (the brain's main inhibitory system) and inhibits NMDA receptors (the brain's main excitatory system). Chronic heavy drinking causes the brain to adapt:

- **GABA receptors downregulate.** Less inhibition.
- **NMDA receptors upregulate.** More excitation.

When alcohol is suddenly removed, the brain is left with too little inhibition (the GABA system is suppressed) and too much excitation (the NMDA system is running hot). This is withdrawal.

| Time After Last Drink | Symptoms | Mechanism |
|----------------------|----------|-----------|
| 6-12 hours | Tremor, anxiety, tachycardia, diaphoresis, insomnia | Sympathetic overdrive from unopposed NMDA activity |
| 12-24 hours | Alcoholic hallucinosis (visual, tactile) | Dopaminergic dysregulation in mesolimbic pathway |
| **24-48 hours** | **Withdrawal seizures** (generalized tonic-clonic) | NMDA-mediated glutamatergic storm in cortex |
| **48-72 hours** | **Delirium tremens** — confusion, agitation, fever, severe autonomic instability | Global neurotransmitter chaos; mortality 5-15% untreated |

**This patient is at 79 hours (3.3 days).** He is squarely in the DTs window. His heart rate of 108 is autonomic instability. He needs benzodiazepines NOW, and he needs monitoring.

### The CIWA-Ar Protocol

The Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol (CIWA-Ar) scores 10 items:
- Nausea/vomiting, tremor, paroxysmal sweats, anxiety, agitation, tactile disturbances, auditory disturbances, visual disturbances, headache, orientation

Each gets a score from 0-7. Total ≤8 = mild (supportive care), 9-15 = moderate (benzodiazepines), ≥16 = severe (aggressive benzodiazepines, possibly ICU). The patient with tachycardia at 108 and distended abdomen likely scores at least 12-15 — moderate to severe.

### Why Benzodiazepines Are the Answer

Benzodiazepines bind to the GABA-A receptor at a different site than alcohol, but they produce the same effect — increased chloride influx, hyperpolarization, decreased neuronal firing. They substitute for the missing alcohol at the GABA receptor. They calm the NMDA storm.

**Chlordiazepoxide (Librium)** is the classic choice — long half-life, smooth taper, less abuse potential than shorter-acting agents. Alternatives: diazepam (also long-acting) or lorazepam (better in liver disease because no active metabolites, but shorter duration).

## What Was Over-Ordered — Why They Don't Belong

### 1. Serum Magnesium — Give It, Don't Check It

**The first-principles point:** Every chronic alcoholic is magnesium-depleted. Period. Here's why:

1. **Poor intake.** Alcoholics replace food with alcohol. Magnesium-rich foods (leafy greens, nuts, whole grains, legumes) disappear from the diet.
2. **Renal magnesium wasting.** Alcohol directly inhibits renal tubular magnesium reabsorption. Even with low serum levels, the kidneys keep dumping magnesium.
3. **GI losses.** Diarrhea and vomiting from alcohol-related gastritis/gastroenteritis deplete magnesium further.
4. **Low serum Mg = low total body Mg.** By the time serum magnesium drops, total body stores are depleted by 1-2 mEq/kg.

**Why checking doesn't change management:** You are going to give magnesium regardless. The reasons are:
- **Withdrawal.** Magnesium is a cofactor for GABA receptor function. Magnesium deficiency worsens withdrawal severity.
- **Arrhythmia prevention.** Alcoholics have prolonged QT. Hypomagnesemia prolongs QT further. Torsades risk.
- **Refeeding syndrome prevention.** Thiamine, B12, and folate push potassium, magnesium, and phosphate into cells during refeeding. If you don't replace magnesium, it crashes.
- **Seizure threshold.** Magnesium stabilizes neuronal membranes. Hypomagnesemia lowers the seizure threshold — exactly when the patient is already at risk for withdrawal seizures.

**The rule:** Magnesium level does not guide you. If it's normal, you give magnesium anyway (the serum level doesn't reflect tissue stores). If it's low, you give magnesium. Either way, you give magnesium. So don't order the level — just replace it empirically (2-4 g IV MgSO4 over 1-2 hours, then oral supplementation).

### 2. Type and Screen — No Bleeding, No Surgery

**The first-principles point:** A type and screen (T&S) determines ABO/Rh blood type and screens for unexpected antibodies. It is ordered when there is a REASONABLE CHANCE the patient will need a blood transfusion in the next 72 hours.

**When T&S is indicated:**
- Active bleeding (GI bleed, trauma, ruptured AAA)
- Planned surgery with >500 mL expected blood loss
- Severe anemia (Hb <7) requiring transfusion
- Coagulopathy with active bleeding
- Pregnancy with Rh-negative mother

**When T&S is NOT indicated:**
- Routine admission for alcohol withdrawal
- No active bleeding
- No planned surgery
- No severe anemia

**The patient had none of the indications.** He has chronic alcoholism and withdrawal — not a GI bleed. Unless you see melena or hematemesis, or his Hgb is <7, T&S is a waste. It costs money, takes nursing time, and — most importantly — it creates a record that suggests you thought he might need blood. If you don't actually think he needs blood, don't create that record.

### 3. Serum Amylase — No Pancreatitis Symptoms

**The first-principles point:** Serum amylase is a marker for pancreatic inflammation. It rises in acute pancreatitis (3x upper limit of normal within 6-12 hours). It can also be elevated in:
- Chronic pancreatitis (mild elevation)
- Salivary gland disease (parotitis)
- Macroamylasemia (amylase bound to immunoglobulin, benign)
- Renal failure (decreased clearance)
- Perforated ulcer, intestinal obstruction, ruptured ectopic (nonspecific)

**The patient did not have pancreatitis symptoms.** No epigastric pain radiating to the back. No nausea/vomiting. No Cullen sign or Grey Turner sign. The abdominal distention is ascites from portal hypertension, not pancreatitis.

**Why you might THINK to order it:** Chronic alcoholics get pancreatitis. It's the second most common cause of acute pancreatitis (after gallstones). So it's not a terrible thought. But you don't order it without symptoms. The patient came in for fatigue, memory trouble, and loss of appetite — not abdominal pain.

**The rule:** Amylase is indicated when the patient has epigastric pain. Without epigastric pain, it's a fishing expedition.

### 4. Serum Osmolality — Wrong Timing

**The first-principles point:** Serum osmolality can be useful in hyponatremia workup, but it only makes sense when:
- You have confirmed hyponatremia on labs
- You need to distinguish isotonic hyponatremia (pseudohyponatremia from hyperlipidemia/hyperproteinemia), hypertonic hyponatremia (hyperglycemia, mannitol), and hypotonic hyponatremia (true hyponatremia)
- Or you suspect a toxic alcohol (methanol, ethylene glycol) with an elevated osmolar gap

**The problem:** You need the BMP sodium result FIRST to know if the patient even has hyponatremia. Then you need to calculate the osmolar gap (measured osmolality - calculated osmolality). Ordering osmolality before you have the sodium and glucose values is like checking a troponin before you have an EKG — you don't know what question you're asking.

**Calculated serum osmolality** = 2(Na) + Glucose/18 + BUN/2.8 + EtOH/4.6
**Measured osmolality** comes from the lab (freezing point depression method).

If the gap (measured - calculated) is >10, consider toxic alcohols, mannitol, or contrast dye.

**The right sequence for this patient:**
1. BMP → find the sodium
2. If Na <135 → calculate osmolality
3. Check urine osmolality and urine sodium
4. THEN order serum osmolality if the clinical picture is unclear

You ordered serum osmolality before knowing if he even had hyponatremia. That's the timing error.

### The "Dilution" — Why This Patient Is Hyponatremic

You kept saying "dilution." That's dilutional hyponatremia — hypervolemic hyponatremia from cirrhosis. Here's the mechanism:

1. **Portal hypertension** from alcoholic cirrhosis → splanchnic vasodilation (nitric oxide overproduction) → decreased effective circulating volume
2. **Decreased effective volume** → carotid baroreceptors sense hypovolemia → ADH (vasopressin) release from posterior pituitary
3. **ADH** → water retention in collecting ducts (aquaporin-2 insertion) → water dilutes the serum sodium
4. **Ascites** → third-spacing of fluid → further decreases effective circulating volume → more ADH → more water retention → lower sodium

**This is NOT sodium depletion. This is WATER EXCESS.** Giving normal saline makes it worse — you're adding more water (NS is 0.9% Na, but the kidney retains the water and dumps the sodium). Treatment for dilutional hyponatremia in cirrhosis:
- **Fluid restriction** (1-1.5 L/day)
- **Treat the underlying liver disease** — abstinence
- **Avoid NS boluses** — they expand volume transiently then make ascites and hyponatremia worse
- **Consider diuretics** (spironolactone + furosemide) for ascites management
- **Vaptans** (tolvaptan, conivaptan) block V2 receptors — but only in hospital, and only for severe symptomatic hyponatremia (Na <120 with seizures/AMS)

## What Was Got Right

- **Diagnosis of alcohol withdrawal** — correct at 79 hours post-cessation
- **Chlordiazepoxide (Librium)** — right benzodiazepine for withdrawal
- **Thiamine before anything else** — prevents Wernicke's encephalopathy (confusion, ataxia, ophthalmoplegia)
- **Folate and B12** — both depleted in chronic alcoholism
- **CBC, BMP, LFTs, PT/PTT** — appropriate liver and hematologic workup
- **TSH** — rule out hyperthyroidism as cause of tachycardia
- **CXR, ECG** — appropriate cardiopulmonary screening
- **Alcohol cessation counseling** — treating the disease, not just the episode

## What May Have Been Missed

- **Abdominal ultrasound** — for ascites, liver texture (cirrhosis vs fatty liver), portal vein flow, spleen size, ruling out HCC
- **Hepatitis panel** — alcoholics have higher rates of HBV and HCV
- **CIWA protocol documentation** — standardized scoring for withdrawal severity, guides benzodiazepine dosing
- **Ammonia level** — if concern for hepatic encephalopathy (the memory trouble could be lactulose-responsive HE, not just alcohol-related cognitive decline)

## Attending's Summary

The diagnosis was right. The core treatment — benzodiazepines + thiamine + fluids — was right. The four over-ordered tests teach a single lesson: order what answers a specific question you have, not what "might be useful."

Magnesium: you're going to give it anyway, so don't check the level.
Type and screen: there's no bleeding, so don't pretend there might be.
Amylase: there's no epigastric pain, so don't fish for pancreatitis.
Osmolality: get the sodium first, THEN decide if you need calculated and measured osms.

And the liver — that distended abdomen is portal hypertension with ascites. That sodium is being diluted by ADH from a cirrhotic liver that can't clear it. Fluid restrict, don't flood with NS.

**The golden rule for alcohol withdrawal:** Benzodiazepines first. Thiamine first. Then the liver. Then stop ordering tests that don't change what you're going to do.
