## Hook

A woman in her 60s trips over a rug. She catches herself but her foot hurts. She comes to your office. You order an ankle X-ray. It doesn't capture the metatarsals. You order a lumbar CT. Normal. The case times out. You scored 15%.

A fracture from tripping in a postmenopausal woman is never just a fracture. It is a bone screaming that it has no calcium left.

---

## Mechanism — Why Fragile Bones Break from Nothing

### The Osteoporosis Cascade

1. **Estrogen withdrawal at menopause.** Estrogen suppresses osteoclast activity through RANKL/OPG signaling. When estrogen drops, RANKL rises. Osteoclasts go unchecked. Bone resorption outpaces bone formation. This is why postmenopausal women lose 2-3% of bone mass per year for the first 5-7 years after menopause.

2. **Trabecular bone goes first.** Osteoporosis hits trabecular (spongy) bone before cortical bone. Trabecular bone has higher surface area and turnover rate. The vertebrae, femoral neck, and distal radius are trabecular-rich and fracture first. The metatarsals also have significant trabecular content — a foot fracture from tripping is a sentinel event.

3. **Vitamin D deficiency accelerates the loss.** Vitamin D is needed for intestinal calcium absorption. Without it, only 10-15% of dietary calcium is absorbed. The parathyroid glands sense the low serum calcium and secrete PTH. PTH pulls calcium from bone. Chronic secondary hyperparathyroidism from vitamin D deficiency is a silent bone thief that operates alongside postmenopausal osteoporosis.

4. **The fragility fracture is a predictor, not an endpoint.** A woman who fractures her metatarsal from tripping has the same bone pathology as the woman who will fracture her hip next year. The foot fracture is the warning. DEXA confirms the T-score. Treatment prevents the hip fracture.

### The Diagnostic Algorithm

| Step | Order | Why |
|------|-------|-----|
| 1 | **Foot X-ray** (not ankle) | Ankle X-ray does not reliably visualize metatarsals. You ordered ankle. This was the wrong study. |
| 2 | **Bone densitometry (DEXA)** | Quantifies bone mineral density. T-score ≤ -2.5 = osteoporosis. This is the screening test. |
| 3 | **Vitamin D 25-OH** | Low vitamin D worsens osteoporosis and must be repleted before or with bisphosphonates. |
| 4 | **CBC + CMP** | CBC: rule out multiple myeloma (anemia, rouleaux). CMP: calcium, alkaline phosphatase, renal function. You did these — correct. |

### The Treatment Ladder

| Step | Order | Why |
|------|-------|-----|
| 1 | **Vitamin D supplementation** | Replete deficiency. 800-2000 IU/day. |
| 2 | **Oral bisphosphonate** (alendronate 70 mg weekly, first-line) | Binds hydroxyapatite in bone. Taken up by osteoclasts during resorption. Induces osteoclast apoptosis. Net effect: reduced bone resorption. Reduces vertebral fracture risk by 40-50%, hip fracture by 30-40%. |
| 3 | **Exercise counseling** | Weight-bearing exercise stimulates osteoblast activity through mechanical loading (Wolff's law). |

## The Attending's Summary

You saw a foot fracture and stayed in the ortho lane. Ankle X-ray. Lumbar CT. Both in the wrong anatomical neighborhood. The real problem was two layers deeper: **this bone should not have broken from tripping.** The fracture was a symptom. Osteoporosis was the disease.

**The pattern:** You fixate on the presenting injury and don't ask *why.* A postmenopausal woman with a fragility fracture is a metabolic bone disease case, not an ortho case. The foot X-ray diagnoses the fracture. The DEXA diagnoses the disease. The bisphosphonate prevents the hip fracture that's coming next.

**Your mantra for the next musculoskeletal case in a woman over 50:** Fracture from standing height → DEXA → vitamin D → bisphosphonate. Four steps. Memorize them.
