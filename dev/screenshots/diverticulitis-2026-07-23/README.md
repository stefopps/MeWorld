# Acute Diverticulitis — Attending Walkthrough

**Date:** 2026-07-23 | **Score:** 44.8% (Avg 68.2%) | **Patient:** 50-year-old male

---

## Hook

He's 50. LLQ pain. Fever. Nausea. Vomiting. Tachycardic at 115.

Diverticulosis is not diverticulitis. The difference is the suffix. -osis means the pouches exist. -itis means one of them is infected. Diverticulosis is common and usually asymptomatic. Diverticulitis is a medical emergency. Most people with diverticulosis never get diverticulitis. You need to know why one pouch lights up.

---

## Mechanism: Osis → Itis

A diverticulum is a mucosal and submucosal herniation through the muscularis propria. It forms at the point of least resistance — where the vasa recta penetrate the colonic wall. These are false diverticula: mucosa + submucosa only, no muscular layer.

The sigmoid colon generates the highest intraluminal pressure in the entire GI tract — up to 90 mmHg during peristalsis. That's because the sigmoid has the narrowest lumen and the thickest muscular wall. Laplace's law: pressure is inversely proportional to radius. Small tube, big pressure.

When a diverticulum forms, a pellet of stool — a fecalith — can lodge inside the pouch. The fecalith obstructs the neck. Mucus production continues but can't drain. Bacteria proliferate in the static environment. The thin wall — remember, no muscularis — perforates. First a microperforation. Then an abscess.

This patient had a 2 cm pericolic abscess. Microperforation with contained infection. If it had been 4 cm, he'd need percutaneous drainage. Above that, surgery.

---

## Spatial Logic: LLQ Pain

The sigmoid colon sits in the left lower quadrant. When inflamed, the pain localizes to the LLQ.

But here's the trick: the inflamed sigmoid sits on the bladder. It irritates the bladder wall. The patient develops dysuria, frequency, sterile pyuria on UA. You think pyelonephritis. But the UA has WBCs and no bacteria. The source is outside the bladder — contiguous inflammation from the sigmoid. Get the CT.

---

## Connecting Thread: NPO = Bowel Rest

Every wave of peristalsis generates 90 mmHg in the sigmoid. Every mmHg pushes bacteria through the microperforation into the pericolic space. You want to stop that.

NPO is not about "preparing for possible surgery." It's about turning off the pressure pump. No food → no peristaltic waves → no pressure spikes → the perforation has a chance to seal.

Same logic makes colonoscopy contraindicated acutely. Insufflating air into an inflamed colon with a microperforation is asking for free air. Wait 4-6 weeks.

---

## Contrast: Uncomplicated vs Complicated

**Uncomplicated:** Inflammation confined to the wall. No abscess. Treat with oral antibiotics outpatient.

**Complicated:** Abscess, free perforation, fistula, obstruction, or phlegmon. This case had a 2 cm abscess. Admit. IV antibiotics (ciprofloxacin + metronidazole, or pip/tazo). NPO. Serial exams. CT-guided drainage if >4 cm.

---

## Clinical Anchors

**CT with IV contrast is the gold standard.** Identifies abscess location and size, rules out free air, detects alternatives. X-ray cannot do this.

**Blood cultures before antibiotics.** Fever + abscess = bacteremia risk. Draw before the first dose.

**Ciprofloxacin + metronidazole** covers gram-negatives AND anaerobes. The colon is an anaerobic environment. Don't use monotherapy that misses anaerobes.

**High fiber diet** for long-term prevention. Larger, softer stools → lower intraluminal pressure → less herniation.
