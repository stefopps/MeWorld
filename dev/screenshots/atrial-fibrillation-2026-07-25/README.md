## Hook

She's 65, feeling her heart race, chest tight, dizzy. She has a history of anxiety — she's on alprazolam PRN. She's been under stress lately. It would be easy to call this a panic attack and send her home.

But she also has hypertension. And the EKG shows atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response. And her CHA2DS2-VASc score is 3.

You did rate control perfectly. IV metoprolol worked — her heart rate dropped, her symptoms improved, she spontaneously converted to sinus rhythm. You consulted cardiology. You ruled out hyperthyroidism. You got the echo showing mild LA enlargement without thrombus.

And then you stopped. You never started anticoagulation. You never got the PT/PTT that would have told you she was safe to anticoagulate. You never got the BMP that would have told you which DOAC to pick and at what dose. You never got the CBC that would have stratified her bleeding risk.

The arrhythmia is managed. The stroke risk is not. The most dangerous thing about AF is not the rapid ventricular rate. It's the clot that forms in the left atrial appendage while you're not looking.

---

## Mechanism: The Atrium That Can't Contract

In sinus rhythm, the atria contract. This atrial kick contributes about 20-25% of cardiac output at rest and up to 40% during exercise. More importantly, it empties the left atrial appendage — a blind pouch off the left atrium that is a natural thrombus incubator.

In atrial fibrillation, the atria don't contract. They fibrillate — quiver at 400-600 impulses per minute. The AV node blocks most of these, passing through only some to the ventricles. The ventricular rate becomes irregularly irregular. But the atria themselves are just twitching. No coordinated contraction. No emptying. 

Blood pools in the left atrial appendage. Stasis. Endothelial activation. Fibrin deposition. A clot forms.

When the atria eventually contract again — whether spontaneously (as this patient did) or with cardioversion — that clot can be ejected into the systemic circulation. If it goes to the brain: stroke. If it goes to a limb: acute ischemia. If it goes to the gut: mesenteric ischemia.

Rate control manages the ventricular response. Anticoagulation prevents the clot. These are two separate problems that require two separate treatments.

---

## Spatial Logic: The Left Atrial Appendage

The left atrial appendage is a remnant of the embryonic left atrium. It's a trabeculated, narrow-necked pouch that sits anterior to the left upper pulmonary vein. In sinus rhythm, contraction empties it completely. In AF, blood stagnates in those trabeculations.

This is why a transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) is the study of choice to rule out LAA thrombus before cardioversion. TTE can miss LAA thrombus — the appendage is posterior, and the transthoracic windows don't visualize it well. Your echo report said "no LAA thrombus" but that was likely from a TTE, which has limited sensitivity for LAA clot.

The LA enlargement on echo is significant. A mildly dilated left atrium means this AF has been brewing for a while — perhaps paroxysmal episodes she didn't notice, perhaps undiagnosed. The enlarged atrium is both a consequence of AF (electrical and structural remodeling) and a contributor to its persistence (more tissue to sustain reentrant circuits). This is "AF begets AF" — the atrium that isn't contracting remodels, and the remodeled atrium is more likely to fibrillate.

## Contrast: Rate Control vs Rhythm Control vs Stroke Prevention

These are three separate decisions in AF management, and they are often confused:

| Goal | When | How |
|------|------|-----|
| **Rate control** | ALL symptomatic AF with RVR | Beta-blocker (metoprolol) or CCB (diltiazem/verapamil) |
| **Rhythm control** | Unstable patients (shock, acute HF) OR elective for recurrent symptomatic AF | Urgent DCCV for unstable; elective cardioversion or ablation for recurrent |
| **Stroke prevention** | CHA2DS2-VASc >=2 (men) or >=3 (women) | DOAC (first-line) or warfarin. NOT aspirin. NOT DAPT. |

You did rate control perfectly. You should not have done rhythm control at this stage — the patient was stable, she converted spontaneously. The ablation order was inappropriate specifically because rhythm control is elective for recurrent AF, not an ED order for new-onset AF.

The miss: stroke prevention. CHA2DS2-VASc 3 = yearly stroke risk ~3-5%. DOAC reduces this by 60-70%. Aspirin reduces it by 20-25%. The guidelines are unambiguous: OAC, not antiplatelets.

## Connecting Thread: Why Ablation Was the Wrong Order

You ordered "Ablation, tumor." This cost you the entire Appropriate Orders score (0%).

Ablation — typically pulmonary vein isolation — is an electrophysiology procedure. A catheter is threaded into the left atrium, and the tissue around the pulmonary vein ostia is ablated (burned or frozen) to electrically isolate the triggers that initiate AF. It's highly effective for paroxysmal AF. It's also invasive, carries risks (cardiac perforation, stroke, PV stenosis, phrenic nerve injury), requires anticoagulation before and after, and is NOT an emergency department intervention.

Ablation is for the patient who comes to clinic three months later saying "I've had three more episodes of AF since I left the hospital, and the metoprolol is working but I don't want to be on it forever." It's an elective decision made by the patient and their electrophysiologist after a trial of medical therapy.

Ordering it in the ED for new-onset AF is like ordering a Whipple procedure for newly diagnosed pancreatic cancer in the ED. It's the right concept — rhythm control — at the wrong time, by the wrong person, in the wrong setting.

## Clinical Anchors

**Rate control + anticoagulation = the AF bundle.** These are the two things every AF patient needs. Rate control manages symptoms. Anticoagulation prevents stroke. Rhythm control is a third decision that comes later, electively, for selected patients.

**CHA2DS2-VASc drives the AC decision.** Score 0 (men) or 1 (women): no AC. Score 1 (men) or 2 (women): consider AC. Score >=2 (men) or >=3 (women): OAC strongly recommended. This patient: 3. OAC is not optional.

**DOACs are first-line.** Apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban, dabigatran. Fixed dosing. No INR monitoring. Fewer drug interactions. Less intracranial hemorrhage than warfarin. Equivalent or superior efficacy. The USMLE now accepts DOACs.

**PT/PTT before starting AC.** Baseline coagulation. Not for heparin bridging (new-onset AF without prior VTE doesn't need a bridge). Just to know what the baseline is before you alter it.

**BMP for renal function.** CrCl determines which DOAC is appropriate and at what dose. CrCl <15: avoid all DOACs. CrCl 15-29: apixaban only (reduced dose). Edoxaban and rivaroxaban need dose adjustment for CrCl 15-50.

**Echo sooner or later.** Not urgent in stable new-onset AF — won't change the immediate decision to rate-control and anticoagulate. But necessary for longer-term planning. Mild LAE is a structural marker that this AF is not a one-time event.

**Hyperthyroidism is a reversible cause of AF.** TSH is mandatory. This patient's thyroid was normal — but you checked, which is correct.
