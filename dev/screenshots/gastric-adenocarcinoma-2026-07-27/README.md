## Mechanism

### The Correa Cascade: From gastritis to cancer in four steps

Picture the gastric mucosa as a field of tall columnar cells packed tightly together, secreting acid and mucus in a perfectly regulated cycle. Something disrupts that cycle. For the intestinal type of gastric adenocarcinoma -- which is what this patient most likely has -- that "something" is usually *H. pylori*.

**Step 1: Superficial gastritis.** H. pylori burrows into the mucus layer and attaches to epithelial cells. It secretes urease, which converts urea to ammonia, neutralizing stomach acid in its immediate vicinity. The immune system responds with neutrophils and lymphocytes. The stomach lining is inflamed. The patient has dyspepsia. This is what the CCS history called "prior gastritis."

**Step 2: Atrophic gastritis.** Years of chronic inflammation destroy the acid-producing parietal cells and the pepsinogen-producing chief cells. The oxyntic glands atrophy. The stomach loses its ability to produce acid and intrinsic factor. The pH rises. This creates an environment where other bacteria can colonize. The patient may now have B12 deficiency from loss of intrinsic factor. Iron absorption, which requires an acidic environment to convert Fe3+ to Fe2+, is impaired.

**Step 3: Intestinal metaplasia.** The stomach, trying to protect itself from the chronic injury, replaces its native columnar epithelium with intestinal-type epithelium -- goblet cells, absorptive cells, Paneth cells. This is metaplasia: one adult cell type replacing another. It's an adaptive response, but it comes with risk. The new intestinal cells carry different gene expression patterns. They're more susceptible to further mutation.

**Step 4: Dysplasia → Adenocarcinoma.** Accumulated mutations (p53, APC, KRAS, microsatellite instability) in the metaplastic epithelium push cells toward uncontrolled growth. The cells lose polarity. They invade the basement membrane. They form a mass. The mass ulcerates. The ulcer bleeds.

That bleeding is what you're seeing: coffee-ground emesis (blood altered by gastric acid), occult blood in stool, microcytic anemia from chronic iron loss. The epigastric pain is the ulcerated tumor. The weight loss and early satiety are from the mass occupying gastric volume and the catabolic state of malignancy.

### Why iron deficiency anemia in gastric cancer

Three mechanisms converge:

1. **Chronic blood loss from the ulcerated tumor.** The tumor outgrows its blood supply. The surface ulcerates. It oozes blood continuously. Over months, this depletes iron stores.
2. **Decreased iron absorption.** Atrophic gastritis raises gastric pH. Iron needs an acidic environment (pH < 3) to be reduced from Fe3+ (ferric) to Fe2+ (ferrous), the only form the duodenal enterocyte can absorb via DMT1.
3. **Decreased dietary intake.** Early satiety from the mass. Anorexia from the malignancy. The patient simply eats less iron.

The CBC shows microcytic, hypochromic anemia. Iron studies confirm: low serum iron, low ferritin, high TIBC (the body screaming for iron), low transferrin saturation.

### The sequence you got wrong

You ordered pantoprazole and PT/PTT before completing diagnostic testing. The CCS algorithm penalized this. The correct sequence is: diagnose first, then treat. EGD with biopsy confirms adenocarcinoma. Then you stage with CT chest/abdomen/pelvis. Then pre-op labs. Then treatment: PPI for symptom relief, surgery consult, oncology consult, NPO/IVF, iron supplementation, cancer counseling.

## What You Missed (8 Gaps)

### 1. EGD with Biopsy -- The Gold Standard (Diagnosis: 51.06%)

CT shows a mass. EGD with biopsy tells you what the mass is. The scope visualizes the tumor directly: ulcerated, irregular, friable. Biopsy forceps take tissue. Pathology reads "invasive adenocarcinoma, intestinal type." Without EGD, you're treating a CT finding, not a tissue diagnosis. This is the single biggest miss -- and it's what dragged your diagnostic score down.

**The mechanism (from the attending):** CT tells you there's a mass. But a mass in the stomach could be lymphoma, GIST, benign ulcer, or adenocarcinoma. Each has completely different treatment. You don't give FLOT chemotherapy for a GIST. You don't resect lymphoma. The biopsy is the fork in the road. Without it, every treatment decision is a guess.

### 2. H. pylori Testing

H. pylori is the initiating event in the Correa cascade for intestinal-type gastric adenocarcinoma. Testing guides two things: (1) antibiotic eradication to prevent further damage to the remaining stomach, and (2) risk stratification for the patient's family. Options: serum antibody, stool antigen, urea breath test, or biopsy urease test (if you're doing EGD anyway).

### 3. CT Chest -- Complete Staging

You scanned the abdomen and pelvis. Good. But gastric cancer metastasizes to lung and mediastinal nodes. A CT chest rules out pulmonary metastases. Complete staging (chest + abdomen + pelvis) is standard of care before any treatment decision.

### 4. Iron Studies (TIBC, Iron, Ferritin)

You ordered CBC. You saw the microcytic anemia. But you didn't confirm it's iron deficiency. Iron studies close the loop: low iron, low ferritin, high TIBC, low transferrin saturation. This guides replacement therapy and distinguishes iron deficiency from anemia of chronic disease (where ferritin is normal or high).

### 5. NPO

She needs an EGD. NPO before the scope. Also before surgery if it goes that route. This is a pre-procedure order that takes one second.

### 6. IV Fluids

NPO + awaiting procedures + anemic + catabolic malignancy = she needs maintenance fluids. Normal saline or LR.

### 7. Iron Supplementation

She has iron deficiency anemia from chronic GI blood loss. Ferrous sulfate 325 mg TID. This treats the anemia while the surgeons and oncologists plan definitive management of the tumor.

### 8. Hematology/Oncology Consult

You consulted surgery and gastroenterology. But gastric adenocarcinoma requires medical oncology for adjuvant chemotherapy (FLOT: fluorouracil, leucovorin, oxaliplatin, docetaxel). Post-gastrectomy, she needs this. The oncology consult should be placed alongside the surgery consult, not after.

## What You Over-Ordered

### Sequencing Error (not a wrong order -- wrong timing)

You ordered pantoprazole and PT/PTT before completing diagnostic testing. Neither is wrong to order. But in CCS, treatment orders before a confirmed diagnosis cost points. The algorithm expects: examine → test (CBC, CMP, EGD, CT) → diagnose → treat (PPI, surgery, oncology).

The pantoprazole itself was correct. The PT/PTT was correct. Just do them after the EGD result.

### Optional: LDH

You listed LDH as one of your orders. LDH is a non-specific marker of cell turnover. It can be elevated in many cancers (lymphoma, germ cell tumors, some solid tumors) but it's not standard for gastric adenocarcinoma workup. Not harmful -- just not needed. The CCS didn't penalize it and didn't list it as a specific miss.
