The patient has traveler's diarrhea from a bacterial infection (E. Coli, most likely enterotoxigenic
strain) that he probably picked up from international travel to Central America. The patient can be
started on an empiric antibiotic (Azithromycin or a fluoroquinolone, such as ciprofloxacin) before the
culture has returned and can remain on this since culture results demonstrated sensitivity to both
options. An additional option to consider would be an anti-motility agent, such as loperamide, which
can help symptomatically but will not treat the underlying infection causing the diarrhea, so should

definitely not be used as monotherapy.

An important distinction must be made regarding antibiotic use in E. coli diarrheal illness. Antibiotics
are contraindicated in Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC, e.g. O157:H7) because they trigger
increased toxin release, raising the risk of hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS). However, this case
involves enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC), the most common causative organism in traveler's diarrhea,
for which antibiotics are appropriate and guideline-supported in moderate-to-severe illness. This
patient meets clear criteria for antibiotic treatment: greater than 6 stools per day (severe), more than
7 days duration (persistent), and symptoms that are incapacitating and interfering with normal
activities. Current CDC and IDSA guidelines support azithromycin or a fluoroquinolone for ETEC
traveler's diarrhea meeting these thresholds, with azithromycin increasingly preferred given rising

fluoroquinolone resistance rates worldwide.

Although most cases of traveler's diarrhea do not require antibiotic treatment and resolve within a
few days, this patient has had severe (>6 stools/day), persistent diarrhea (>7 days) that is
incapacitating and very much interfering with normal planned activities. Other indications for
antibiotic treatment (which this patient does NOT have) are fever and blood, pus, or mucus in the
stool. In this case, the patient will continue to have diarrhea if left untreated but will improve once
antibiotics are initiated. It would also be important to counsel the patient about maintaining

adequate hydration with fluids and electrolyte repletion.
