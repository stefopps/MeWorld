## Mechanism: The Hernia That Shouldn't Be There

The esophageal hiatus is an opening in the diaphragm at T10. The esophagus passes through it, and the phrenoesophageal membrane anchors the gastroesophageal junction in place. This membrane is a fibroelastic sheet that attaches the esophagus to the diaphragm — it's strong enough to hold the stomach below the diaphragm while allowing the esophagus to slide a few centimeters during swallowing.

In a Type I (sliding) hiatal hernia, the phrenoesophageal membrane weakens. Increased intra-abdominal pressure — from obesity, pregnancy, ascites, chronic cough — pushes the gastric cardia upward through the hiatus into the chest. The GE junction moves above the diaphragm. The result: the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) loses its diaphragmatic crural support.

The crural diaphragm normally pinches the LES from the outside, reinforcing the sphincter's own tone. When the GE junction herniates, that pinch is lost. The LES tone drops. Acid refluxes freely from the stomach into the unprotected esophagus. Heartburn. Regurgitation. Over time, chronic acid exposure causes esophagitis, stricture formation, and in some patients, Barrett's esophagus.

That's why dysphagia matters. It means the esophagus is no longer just inflamed — it's narrowed. Food physically cannot pass. That narrowing could be a benign peptic stricture. Or it could be a malignancy. You cannot tell without looking.

## Spatial Logic: Above vs Below the Diaphragm

The diaphragm divides the thorax from the abdomen. In a normal person, the GE junction sits at or just below the diaphragmatic hiatus. The lower 2-3 cm of the esophagus is intra-abdominal, exposed to positive abdominal pressure. This positive pressure helps keep the LES closed — the pressure gradient between the abdomen (+5 to +10 mmHg) and the thorax (-5 mmHg) compresses the distal esophagus.

When a hiatal hernia slides the GE junction above the diaphragm, the distal esophagus is now in the negative-pressure thorax. The pressure gradient reverses: the stomach (below the diaphragm, +5 to +10 mmHg) pushes against the LES, but the LES is now above the diaphragm at -5 mmHg thoracic pressure. The result is a 10-15 mmHg pressure difference favoring reflux. The LES tone alone cannot overcome this gradient.

This is the mechanical basis of GERD in hiatal hernia. It's not just a weak sphincter — it's a sphincter that has lost its anatomical reinforcement and is now fighting a pressure gradient that physics wins every time.

## Contrast: Type I vs Types II-IV — The Management Fork

| Type | Anatomy | % | Treatment |
|------|---------|---|-----------|
| **I (Sliding)** | GE junction + gastric cardia slide above diaphragm | 95% | Medical: PPI, H2 blocker, weight loss, lifestyle |
| **II** | Gastric fundus herniates alongside esophagus (GE junction stays below diaphragm) | ~2% | Surgical |
| **III** | Both Type I (GE junction up) and Type II (fundus up) | ~2% | Surgical |
| **IV** | Other organs herniate — colon, spleen, pancreas, small bowel | <1% | Surgical — risk of strangulation/volvulus |

The problem: you cannot distinguish these by history alone. A Type III hernia can present with the exact same GERD symptoms as Type I. The difference is that Type III can strangulate, perforate, or cause gastric volvulus — and PPI won't prevent any of that.

This is why imaging is mandatory with dysphagia. You need to see where the GE junction sits, whether there's a paraesophageal component, and whether the esophagus has a stricture or mass.

## Connecting Thread: The Three Decisions in Chest Pain

Every chest pain presentation requires three parallel investigations, even when the history strongly points to one system:

1. **Rule out cardiac.** EKG. Troponin if acute. Even if the pain is burning, positional, and associated with meals. CAD and GERD coexist in the same patient population — obesity, age, shared risk factors. The EKG is noninvasive and takes 5 minutes.

2. **Rule out structural GI pathology.** Dysphagia = EGD or barium swallow. Alarm symptoms demand visualization. A PPI trial is for young patients with classic, uncomplicated GERD and no red flags. Dysphagia is a red flag.

3. **Confirm the diagnosis before committing to long-term treatment.** Type I gets PPI. Type II-IV gets surgery. The binary fork cannot be navigated by guesswork. Metronidazole, by the way, has no role in uncomplicated GERD — it's for H. pylori or anaerobic infections. Check why you added it.

You did #3 — sort of. You picked the right drug for the most likely diagnosis. But you skipped #1 entirely and #2 was the whole problem. The score says 13% on diagnosis for a reason: you never confirmed anything.

## Clinical Anchors

- **Dysphagia = alarm symptom.** Always scope. Always image. Never empirically treat.
- **Chest pain = rule out cardiac.** Always EKG. Even in the office. Even when it sounds GI.
- **Type I = medical, Types II-IV = surgical.** Must image to decide.
- **PPI alone is correct for Type I.** But only after you've confirmed it is Type I.
- **Weight loss counseling is treatment.** Obesity drives GERD mechanically. Documenting weight loss advice is preventive care and disease management.
