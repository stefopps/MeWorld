Yet this baby has meningitis. The diagnosis hangs entirely on three physical exam findings that replace the adult triad: bulging fontanelle, nuchal rigidity, and photophobia. You found all three. That is the hard part. What you missed was completing the workup that the triad demands.

## Mechanism: The CSF as a Diagnostic Window

The meninges are not a sterile vacuum. They are an immunologic compartment with its own cellular and biochemical profile. When a pathogen crosses into the subarachnoid space, the CSF becomes a liquid biopsy of that infection.

Picture the choroid plexus secreting CSF at 20 mL/hour in an infant. This fluid bathes the brain and spinal cord, then drains through arachnoid granulations into the venous sinuses. When a virus invades the meninges, lymphocytes cross the blood-CSF barrier and multiply in the subarachnoid space. They do not consume glucose — viral metabolism is negligible. The protein rises modestly from capillary leak but never reaches the grams-per-deciliter of bacterial meningitis.

Now picture bacterial invasion. Neutrophils pour in. They gobble glucose at a ferocious rate — bacterial meningitis CSF glucose drops to less than 40% of serum glucose, sometimes below 10 mg/dL. The protein skyrockets past 100-500 mg/dL from massive blood-brain barrier disruption. Gram stain shows organisms. Culture grows in hours.

The CSF triad — glucose, protein, cell count — tells you which war is being fought in the meninges:

| Parameter | Viral | Bacterial |
|-----------|-------|-----------|
| WBC | 10-500, lymphocytic | 100-10,000+, neutrophilic |
| Glucose | Normal (>40% serum) | Low (<40% serum) |
| Protein | 50-150 mg/dL | 100-500+ mg/dL |
| Gram stain | Negative | Organisms seen |
| Culture | Negative | Positive in 24-48h |

Your patient: WBC 20 (lymphocytic), protein 50, glucose normal. This is a textbook viral meningitis CSF. The virus — likely an enterovirus (coxsackie, echo) in summer — has invaded the meninges, provoked a lymphocytic response, but left the glucose and protein barely disturbed. The blood-brain barrier is intact. The infection is self-limited.

**The clinical anchor:** Normal CSF glucose is the fork in the road. If glucose is normal, the meninges are fighting a virus. You provide supportive care, not antibiotics. If glucose is low, the meninges are fighting bacteria. You start empiric antibiotics within 30 minutes or the infant dies.

In a pre-verbal infant, meningitis presents as fever plus irritability — the same presentation as otitis media, UTI, and a dozen other infant illnesses. ## The Attending's Framework: Infant Fever = Three Compartments

Every febrile infant under 3 months gets a complete septic workup of three sterile compartments:

1. **Blood** — CBC, blood culture
2. **Urine** — UA, urine culture
3. **CSF** — Cell count, glucose, protein, gram stain, culture

If the infant looks sick (toxic appearance, poor feeding, bulging fontanelle), you do all three before antibiotics. If the CSF comes back viral, you stop. If any compartment suggests bacteria, you start empiric antibiotics immediately — ceftriaxone + ampicillin (covers group B strep, E. coli, Listeria) or cefotaxime + ampicillin.

You did compartment 3 well. You neglected compartments 1 and 2. The CCS scoring weights diagnostic orders at 45% of total score — the CBC, UA, urine culture, blood culture, CSF glucose, and CSF gram stain are 6 of the 8 missed items. That is where your 44% diagnosis score came from.

## First Principles

**Why infants under 6 months don't show Kernig or Brudzinski:** These signs test for meningeal stretch — Kernig extends the knee with hip flexed, Brudzinski flexes the neck and watches for hip flexion. Both require a fully myelinated nervous system and sufficient muscle mass to generate resistance. Infants have incomplete myelination, hypotonia, and short limbs — the mechanical setup doesn't exist yet. The infant triad replaces it: bulging fontanelle (open sutures transmit ICP directly), nuchal rigidity (meningeal inflammation is the one sign that translates), and photophobia (optic nerve sheath shares the meningeal inflammation).

**Why normal CSF glucose rules out bacterial meningitis:** Bacteria ferment glucose. Neutrophils consume glucose in their respiratory burst. Together, bacterial metabolism + neutrophil glycolysis drops CSF glucose well below 40% of serum. Viruses do neither — they replicate inside host cells using host ATP, they don't ferment glucose, and the lymphocytic infiltrate is metabolically quiet. Normal CSF glucose in the setting of pleocytosis is, by itself, enough to call it viral.

**Why aspirin kills children with viral illness:** Aspirin inhibits mitochondrial beta-oxidation in hepatocytes. During a viral illness, the liver is already under metabolic stress. Aspirin pushes it over the edge — fatty infiltration, mitochondrial failure, acute hepatic encephalopathy. The brain swells. ICP rises. The child dies. This is Reye syndrome. It is rare but catastrophic, and it is the reason aspirin is contraindicated in any febrile child under 16.
