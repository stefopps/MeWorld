## Hook

A feral cat sinks its teeth into the soft tissue of your patient's leg, then disappears into the alley. Three days later, you are not looking at a puncture wound anymore. You are looking at a polymicrobial battlefield: four different organisms have colonized this bite, and one of them -- Pseudomonas -- has already flipped green.

**The question that determines this patient's outcome is not** "*What antibiotic do I start?*" **It is** "*What else does this bite mandate that antibiotics cannot fix?*"

---

## Mechanism — Why Four Organisms Are Here

### Pasteurella multocida (cat oral flora — always present)

Pasteurella lives in the oropharynx of 70-90% of cats. It is a Gram-negative coccobacillus that uses a virulence polysaccharide capsule to resist phagocytosis. When inoculated deep into tissue by a cat's needle-sharp teeth, it causes a rapidly progressive cellulitis within 12-24 hours -- one of the fastest onsets of any bite pathogen.

**Why Pasteurella matters:** If a cat bite looks infected at 24 hours, it is almost certainly Pasteurella. Ampicillin and beta-lactams cover it well. But Pasteurella is *never alone* in a bite that sat for three days.

### Pseudomonas aeruginosa (secondary invader — environment)

Pseudomonas is not normal cat oral flora. It is an environmental organism (soil, water, sinks, hospital drains) that enters wounds secondarily. In this case, the 3-day delay, moist wound conditions, and necrotic tissue created a perfect Pseudomonas niche.

The green color comes from **pyocyanin** — a blue-green phenazine pigment that Pseudomonas produces through the Phz operon. Pyocyanin is not just cosmetic. It is a virulence factor: it generates reactive oxygen species that damage host tissue, impairs ciliary function, and inhibits lymphocyte proliferation.

**Why you MUST cover Pseudomonas here:** The green discharge signals Pseudomonas colonization. If you pick Augmentin (amoxicillin-clavulanate) — the most common "cat bite antibiotic" — you will fail. Pseudomonas has intrinsic resistance to amoxicillin-clavulanate through three mechanisms:

1. **Chromosomal AmpC beta-lactamase** — not inhibited by clavulanate
2. **Low outer membrane permeability** — beta-lactams cannot penetrate
3. **Efflux pumps (MexAB-OprM)** — actively expels drugs that do get in

This is why pip-tazo works: piperacillin is a strong enough beta-lactam to overcome efflux, AND tazobactam irreversibly inhibits AmpC.

### Bacteroides fragilis (anaerobe — oral flora)

Bacteroides is a Gram-negative anaerobe that thrives in the low-oxygen environment of a deep puncture wound. It produces foul-smelling short-chain fatty acids (butyrate, succinate) and has a potent polysaccharide capsule that is its primary virulence factor — the capsule triggers abscess formation.

**Key treatment point:** Bacteroides is *always* resistant to beta-lactams without beta-lactamase inhibitor coverage. It produces its own beta-lactamases. Metronidazole is the gold standard for anaerobic coverage, but pip-tazo and carbapenems also work.

### Streptococcus anginosus group (oral flora)

The anginosus group (S. anginosus, S. constellatus, S. intermedius) are normal oral commensals. They are microaerophilic and facultatively anaerobic. In deep tissue, they form abscesses — S. intermedius in particular has a strong association with abscess formation in the brain and liver, though here it is in the leg.

## Spatial Logic — Where the Infection Lives

This is a puncture wound on the lower extremity. The cat's teeth drove bacteria through the skin, subcutaneous fat, and possibly into muscle fascia. The depth matters because:

- **Superficial** (subcutaneous only): Erythema, warmth, purulent discharge. Treatable with antibiotics + wound care.
- **Deep / fascial plane involvement**: You worry about necrotizing fasciitis. CT is the imaging of choice to evaluate fascial gas — MRI is too slow in the ER and cannot rule out foreign bodies (tooth fragments).

**Why you need the X-ray/CT (and did not order it):** Cat teeth are small, sharp fracturing needles. Tooth fragments can be retained in deep tissue and act as a nidus for persistent infection. X-ray/CT rules out:
- Retained tooth fragments (radiopaque)
- Subcutaneous gas (NF)
- Foreign bodies
- Baseline bone status (osteomyelitis changes lag 10-21 days, so a normal X-ray today does not rule it out)

## Connecting Thread — Why Rabies and Tetanus Are Not Optional

You treated the infection correctly with pip-tazo. But an animal bite is a **three-threat event**:

| Threat | Mechanism | What you need | Did you do it? |
|--------|-----------|---------------|----------------|
| **Bacterial infection** | Inoculated oral flora + environmental overgrowth | Empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics (cover Pasteurella + Pseudomonas + anaerobes) | Yes (pip-tazo) |
| **Rabies virus** | Rhabdovirus in saliva travels via retrograde axonal transport to CNS at ~12-100 mm/day | HRIG (passive, immediate) + Rabies vaccine (active, 7-14 days to antibodies) | **Missed** |
| **Tetanus** | Clostridium tetani spores in deep puncture wound produce tetanospasmin → irreversible GABA/glycine inhibition | Tetanus booster if >5 years since last dose | **Missed** |

**Rabies is 100% fatal once symptomatic.** There is no recovery, no treatment, no second chance. The incubation period is usually 1-3 months but can be days or years depending on the distance from the inoculation site to the CNS and the viral load.

The stray cat cannot be observed or tested. That means the default is **full PEP**:
1. **HRIG (20 IU/kg)** — infiltrate as much as possible around the wound, remainder IM distant from vaccine site
2. **Rabies vaccine IM** — days 0, 3, 7, 14

**Tetanus:** Cat bites are tetanus-prone wounds. The puncture seals over quickly, creating the anaerobic environment C. tetani needs. Tetanospasmin cleaves synaptobrevin (SNARE protein), permanently blocking inhibitory neurotransmitter release (GABA, glycine). The result is unopposed motor neuron firing — lockjaw, opisthotonos, risus sardonicus. A tetanus booster given now prevents this.

## Zosyn — Why It Is the Correct Empiric Choice Here

Piperacillin-tazobactam is the only single-agent that covers all four pathogens:

| Pathogen | Piperacillin alone | + Tazobactam |
|----------|-------------------|---------------|
| Pasteurella | Covered | — |
| Pseudomonas | Covered (if susceptible) | Tazobactam binds AmpC |
| Bacteroides fragilis | Barely any activity | Tazobactam blocks its beta-lactamases |
| Streptococcus anginosus | Covered | — |

Meropenem is also correct but should be reserved for severe infections / treatment failure. Cefepime + metronidazole/clindamycin is correct but requires two agents.

## Key Takeaway

**Antibiotics treat the infection. Prophylaxis prevents the disasters.** In a cat bite from a stray animal, the three mandatory interventions are:

1. Empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics (cover Pasteurella + Pseudomonas + anaerobes)
2. Rabies PEP (HRIG + vaccine series)
3. Tetanus booster (if >5 years)

Missing #2 or #3 is a fatal error. Missing both means you treated only one-third of the threat.
