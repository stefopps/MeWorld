# Congestive Heart Failure (HFrEF) — Attending Walkthrough

**Date:** 2026-07-25 | **Score:** 64.91% (Avg 70.2%) | **Patient:** 62M professor, 30 pack-years, EF 35%

---

## Hook

A 62-year-old man has been waking up at night gasping for air for three weeks. He sits upright, the breath returns, and he falls back asleep. This is paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea — PND — and it means fluid is redistributing from the legs and splanchnic bed into the pulmonary circulation every time he lies supine. The heart cannot pump what gravity dumps into it.

His CXR shows cardiomegaly, Kerley B lines, pleural effusion. His echo shows EF 35%. He has systolic heart failure from ischemic cardiomyopathy — a prior MI killed enough myocardium that what's left cannot generate sufficient forward flow. The diagnosis was correct. The furosemide and spironolactone were correct. The location was wrong — this is an inpatient admission, not an office visit.

And then you stopped. You never started a beta-blocker before discharge. You never started an SGLT2 inhibitor. You never started a statin for his coronary artery disease. You gave him two of the four pillars of guideline-directed medical therapy. The other two were left on the table.

## Mechanism: The Heart as a Pump — Starling's Law in Failure

The Frank-Starling mechanism is the heart's fundamental contractile law: within physiologic limits, the more the ventricular muscle is stretched during diastole, the more forcefully it contracts during systole. More venous return = more stretch = more stroke volume. This works because stretching the sarcomere increases the overlap between actin and myosin filaments, generating more cross-bridge cycling and more force.

In systolic heart failure, the problem is that the pump cannot generate enough force regardless of how much it is stretched. The Starling curve has shifted downward and rightward. At any given filling pressure, the stroke volume is lower than normal. The ventricle dilates to compensate — more preload, more stretch, more volume — but the failing myocardium cannot convert that stretch into effective contraction. The ejection fraction drops below 40%.

The kidneys sense the reduced forward flow and respond exactly as they should in hypovolemia: they activate the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system. Angiotensin II vasoconstricts. Aldosterone retains sodium and water. The intravascular volume expands. But the heart cannot pump this expanded volume forward. It backs up.

The backward failure explains every symptom this patient has. Fluid accumulates in the pulmonary interstitium — first in the dependent bases during the day, then throughout the lungs when he lies flat at night. The interstitial fluid compresses small airways and activates J-receptors, creating the sensation of dyspnea. When the interstitial pressure exceeds the capacity of the pulmonary lymphatics, fluid transudes into the alveoli and pleural space — Kerley B lines and pleural effusion on CXR.

The forward failure explains why his kidneys are activating RAAS even though he is volume-overloaded. The kidneys do not measure total body volume. They measure effective circulating volume — what the heart is actually delivering to the renal arteries. A failing pump = reduced renal perfusion = RAAS activation = more volume retention = more congestion. This is the vicious cycle of heart failure: the body's attempt to fix the problem makes it worse.

## Spatial Logic: Where the Fluid Goes

Pulmonary edema in CHF follows gravity. During the day, the patient is upright. Fluid accumulates in the lung bases. On exam: bibasilar crackles. At night, he lies flat. Fluid redistributes from the bases throughout both lung fields and from the lower extremities into the central circulation. The result: PND.

The timeline tells you everything. Three weeks of progressive dyspnea. Not acute pulmonary edema from flash failure — no pink frothy sputum, no immediate respiratory collapse. This is subacute decompensation of chronic systolic dysfunction. The ventricle has been failing for a while. The RAAS has been compensating. The fluid has been accumulating slowly. Three weeks is the tipping point — the lymphatic system's reserve capacity has been exhausted.

Kerley B lines on CXR are horizontal lines in the costophrenic angles. They represent interlobular septal thickening — the connective tissue planes between lung lobules becoming visible because they are filled with fluid. They are the earliest radiographic sign of pulmonary venous congestion, visible before frank alveolar edema appears. Cardiomegaly on CXR reflects chronic ventricular dilation — the Starling curve's desperate attempt to recruit more sarcomere stretch.

## Contrast: HFrEF vs HFpEF — Same Symptoms, Different Problem

| Feature | HFrEF (our patient) | HFpEF |
|---------|---------------------|-------|
| EF | <40% (systolic failure) | >=50% (diastolic failure) |
| Problem | Can't squeeze | Can't relax |
| Ventricle | Dilated, thin-walled | Hypertrophied, stiff |
| Mechanism | Contractile dysfunction | Impaired relaxation + increased stiffness |
| CXR | Cardiomegaly | Normal or near-normal heart size |
| GDMT pillars | All four (ACEi/BB/MRA/SGLT2i) | SGLT2i, diuretics for symptoms, treat comorbidities |

Our patient: dilated heart, cardiomegaly on CXR, EF 35%. This is classic systolic failure. The four pillars apply. Every single one of them reduces mortality.

## Connecting Thread: The Four Pillars of GDMT — Why Each One Works

The 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA guidelines define four drug classes that reduce mortality in HFrEF. Each attacks a different node in the heart failure vicious cycle:

**1. ACE inhibitor / ARB / ARNI (Lisinopril — already on it).** Blocks angiotensin II → reduces afterload (vasodilation), reduces preload (less aldosterone → less sodium/water retention), reduces myocardial fibrosis (less angiotensin-II-driven collagen deposition). The CONSENSUS trial showed a 40% mortality reduction with enalapril in severe HF — one of the most dramatic results in cardiovascular medicine.

**2. Beta-blocker (carvedilol / metoprolol succinate / bisoprolol — MISSED).** This is counterintuitive. Why give a negative inotrope to a patient whose heart can't squeeze? Because chronic sympathetic activation is toxic to the failing myocardium. Norepinephrine increases heart rate (more oxygen demand), promotes apoptosis (more myocyte death), and triggers arrhythmias (sudden death). Beta-blockers interrupt this catecholamine toxicity. Start AFTER the patient is diuresed and euvolemic — never during acute decompensation or you risk cardiogenic shock. COPERNICUS, MERIT-HF, CIBIS-II: each showed 34% mortality reduction. Predischarge initiation improves long-term adherence (IMPACT-HF). Some hospitals penalize CHF discharge without a beta-blocker.

**3. Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (Spironolactone — ordered, correct).** Aldosterone has TWO targets, not one. In the kidney: principal cells of the distal tubule and collecting duct — aldosterone binds MR → upregulates ENaC → sodium reabsorption, water retention. That's the diuretic target you already know. But cardiac fibroblasts and vascular smooth muscle ALSO express mineralocorticoid receptors. Aldosterone binding at the heart triggers collagen deposition (myocardial fibrosis), oxidative stress (endothelial damage), and pro-arrhythmic scar remodeling (re-entry circuits → sudden death). This is why RALES showed 30% mortality reduction in patients *already on ACE inhibitors and furosemide.* If spironolactone were just a potassium-sparing diuretic, adding it to furosemide wouldn't save lives — you'd just pee more. The survival benefit comes from cardiac MR blockade. Must check K+ and eGFR before starting — hyperkalemia is the dose-limiting toxicity.

**4. SGLT2 inhibitor (dapagliflozin / empagliflozin — MISSED).** The newest pillar and the most surprising. SGLT2 inhibitors were designed for diabetes. Then DAPA-HF and EMPEROR-Reduced showed 25% reduction in worsening HF or cardiovascular death — in patients WITH and WITHOUT diabetes. The mechanism is incompletely understood but involves osmotic diuresis, improved cardiac energetics (ketone body shift), reduced oxidative stress, and favorable ventricular remodeling. Unlike beta-blockers, SGLT2 inhibitors can be started during hospitalization once the patient is past the acute decompensation phase (EMPULSE trial). This is not optional — it is Class I, Level of Evidence A.

Two of four pillars is not enough. Each one independently reduces mortality. The additive benefit of all four combined is what turns a progressive fatal disease into a chronic manageable condition.

## Clinical Anchors

- **PND + orthopnea + CXR with Kerley B lines = CHF until proven otherwise.** The timeline (3 weeks) tells you subacute decompensation of chronic dysfunction. Not flash pulmonary edema.
- **Acute management: diurese first.** Furosemide IV. Then layer on GDMT sequentially, never simultaneously.
- **Beta-blocker: after diuresis, before discharge.** Never during acute decompensation. Carvedilol is the guideline-preferred agent (COMET).
- **SGLT2 inhibitor: can start inpatient, post-decompensation.** Not just for diabetes anymore. Mortality benefit independent of glucose.
- **Statin: for his CAD, not his CHF.** Prior MI = secondary prevention indication. CORONA showed no mortality benefit of statins in HF alone — but this man has ASCVD.
- **Location: CHF with effusion, PND, EF 35% = inpatient.** Office is for stable outpatient management. This is not that.
- **Vaccinate at discharge.** Pneumococcal (CHF + smoking), Tdap (every 10 years), Shingrix (50+). Do not discharge a CHF patient without their vaccines.
