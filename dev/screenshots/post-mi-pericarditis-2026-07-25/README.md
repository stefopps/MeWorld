## Hook

The ECG shows diffuse ST elevation. PR depression. Classic pericarditis.

The trap is thinking "pericarditis, give ibuprofen, send home." This is a post-MI patient. Ibuprofen kills these patients. Literally. NSAIDs in post-MI patients increase mortality and re-infarction by impairing myocardial scar formation and tipping the endothelial balance toward thrombosis.

You didn't fall for that trap. You correctly avoided every NSAID. You gave aspirin (allowed — it's an antiplatelet, not an anti-inflammatory at these doses) and acetaminophen. Treatment 100%.

What you missed was proving it wasn't a second MI first. Post-MI chest pain = rule out re-infarction. Troponins. CXR. The ECG strongly suggested pericarditis. You were right. But being right without proof in a post-MI patient is just being lucky.

## Mechanism: The Immune System Attacks the Heart It Tried to Heal

During a myocardial infarction, cardiomyocytes die. Their intracellular contents — cardiac antigens that the immune system has never seen in the extracellular space — spill out. The immune system encounters these antigens. It mounts a response.

Days to weeks later, that response finds its way to the pericardium. The pericardium shares antigenic epitopes with the myocardium. Antibodies and immune complexes deposit in the pericardial layers. Complement activates. Neutrophils infiltrate. The pericardium becomes inflamed, edematous, and exquisitely painful.

This is Dressler syndrome — or, if it happens earlier (days rather than weeks), peri-infarct pericarditis. The mechanism is the same: autoimmune inflammation driven by exposure to cardiac antigens. It is NOT infectious. It is NOT ischemic. It is the immune system doing what the immune system always does when exposed to novel antigens — it attacks. It just happens to be attacking the tissue surrounding the heart instead of the bacteria it was designed to fight.

The pain is sharp, pleuritic. It's worse when lying supine because the inflamed pericardium stretches against the posterior chest wall. It improves when sitting forward because the pericardium relaxes and the inflamed layers separate slightly. Positional chest pain is pericarditis until proven otherwise.

## Spatial Logic: Why the ECG Looks Like That

The pericardium is a two-layer sac surrounding the entire heart. When it's inflamed, the inflammation is diffuse — it affects the entire pericardial surface. This is fundamentally different from a myocardial infarction, where necrosis is confined to a single coronary artery's territory.

In STEMI, the ST elevation is territorial: anterior leads (V1-V4) for LAD occlusion, inferior leads (II, III, aVF) for RCA occlusion, lateral leads (I, aVL, V5-V6) for LCx occlusion. There are reciprocal changes — ST depression in the leads opposite the infarct territory.

In pericarditis, the ST elevation is diffuse. It appears in II, III, aVF, V2-V6 — across multiple coronary territories simultaneously. There are no reciprocal changes because there's no territory "opposite" the inflamed pericardium. The entire pericardium is inflamed. Every lead touching it sees the same thing.

And there's PR depression. The atria sit within the pericardium too. Atrial inflammation causes a current of injury during atrial depolarization, which manifests as PR segment depression. This is the single most reliable ECG finding distinguishing pericarditis from STEMI: in STEMI, the PR segment is normal. In pericarditis, it's depressed.

## Contrast: Why Aspirin Is Allowed and Ibuprofen Is Not

The USMLE loves this distinction. Here's the mechanism:

Aspirin irreversibly acetylates COX-1 in platelets. At low doses (81-325 mg), it blocks thromboxane A2 synthesis preferentially — thromboxane A2 promotes platelet aggregation. The result: antiplatelet effect. This is why aspirin is part of every post-MI regimen. It prevents stent thrombosis and recurrent MI.

NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen, indomethacin, celecoxib) reversibly inhibit both COX-1 and COX-2. They block prostaglandin synthesis everywhere — not just in platelets. In the endothelium, COX-2 produces prostacyclin (PGI2), which is vasodilatory and antiplatelet. NSAIDs knock out prostacyclin production in the vessel wall. At the same time, they incompletely suppress platelet thromboxane A2 (because the inhibition is reversible, not permanent like aspirin's). The net effect: a pro-thrombotic state.

In a post-MI patient, that pro-thrombotic state is lethal. The myocardium is trying to scar. The endothelium is trying to heal. NSAIDs interfere with both processes. Multiple large cohort studies show increased mortality and re-infarction rates in post-MI patients given NSAIDs.

Aspirin at antiplatelet doses does not block endothelial COX-2 significantly. It is the one anti-inflammatory that post-MI patients can safely take for pericarditis pain. Acetaminophen works through a completely different mechanism (central COX inhibition, possibly via COX-3 or cannabinoid pathways). It's safe.

## Clinical Anchors

- **Post-MI chest pain = troponin first.** Even when the ECG says pericarditis. You have to prove it's not re-infarction before you call it something else.
- **NSAIDs are contraindicated post-MI.** The one exception is aspirin at antiplatelet doses. Ibuprofen, naproxen, indomethacin — none of them. Not for pericarditis. Not for anything.
- **Diffuse ST elevation + PR depression = pericarditis.** Territorial ST elevation + reciprocal changes + normal PR segment = STEMI. The PR segment is the giveaway.
- **Pericarditis is self-limited.** Most cases resolve in 1-2 weeks with aspirin and rest. No cath lab. No aggressive imaging. Restraint is the correct management.
- **Colchicine is optional in post-MI pericarditis.** It's standard in idiopathic pericarditis (reduces recurrence by 50%). In post-MI, the evidence is thin. The CCS case says optional, not graded.

## What You Missed

| Missed | Impact |
|--------|--------|
| **Cardiac troponin** | Post-MI patient with new chest pain. Rule out re-infarction first. Troponin tells you if there's active myocardial necrosis. Pericarditis alone can cause mild elevation, but a significant rise changes management. |
| **Chest X-ray** | Ruled out pneumothorax, widened mediastinum, pleural effusion. Pericarditis can produce a pericardial effusion — CXR shows an enlarged cardiac silhouette if present. |
| **CBC** | Recent cardiac cath = infection risk. Leukocytosis suggests infectious pericarditis, not autoimmune. |
| **BMP / LFTs** | Basic labs for any admission. Renal function matters for drug dosing. |
| **Rest advice** | Activity re-triggers pericarditis symptoms. Advise rest until symptoms resolve. |
