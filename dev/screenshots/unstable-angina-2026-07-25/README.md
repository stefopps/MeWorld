# Unstable Angina — Attending Walkthrough

**Date:** 2026-07-25 | **Score:** 45.01% (Avg 64.7%) | **Patient:** 65-year-old man

## Hook

He's 65, substernal pressure radiating to his jaw, 8 out of 10. He's having his second or third episode in 24 hours. He describes it like "someone is standing on his chest." He's a smoker. He has hypertension and hyperlipidemia that were diagnosed but never treated — "my doctor said I should take something for my pressures and cholesterol, but I never started."

His EKG shows ST depressions in I, aVL, V2 through V6. Anterolateral ischemia. Troponin is negative. Echo is normal. CXR is normal.

He has unstable angina. You gave him aspirin, nitroglycerin, and called cardiology. Then you waited.

The real treatment never happened. No P2Y12 inhibitor. No heparin. No beta-blocker. No statin. No catheterization. He stayed in the ED for six hours while his chest pain persisted. The case timed out. Treatment orders: 22%.

Calling cardiology is an introduction. Cardiac catheterization is the conversation.

## Mechanism: The Plaque That Didn't Kill Him — Yet

Atherosclerosis is not a plumbing problem. It's a biology problem. Cholesterol particles embed in the arterial wall. Macrophages consume them and become foam cells. The foam cells die, releasing their lipid contents and inflammatory mediators. A fibrous cap forms over this necrotic core. That's a stable plaque.

Unstable angina happens when the fibrous cap **fissures or ruptures.** The cap doesn't fully tear open — if it did, the thrombus would fully occlude the vessel, and you'd have a STEMI. Instead, the cap develops a small crack. Platelets adhere to the exposed subendothelial collagen. They aggregate. They release ADP and thromboxane A2. A small, non-occlusive thrombus forms.

This thrombus partially occludes the coronary artery. Blood flow is reduced — not stopped. The myocardium beyond the stenosis becomes ischemic, especially during demand (tachycardia, hypertension, exertion). That's the chest pain. But no myocytes have died yet — that's why the troponin is zero.

The danger is that this partial thrombus can grow. More platelets aggregate. The coagulation cascade activates — thrombin converts fibrinogen to fibrin. The thrombus extends. The artery goes from 70% to 90% to 100% occluded. Now you have a STEMI. Dead myocardium. Heart failure. Arrhythmia. Death.

The ACS bundle exists to interrupt this cascade at every possible point:

| Drug | Target | Mechanism |
|------|--------|-----------|
| **Aspirin** | COX-1 → thromboxane A2 | Blocks one platelet activation pathway |
| **P2Y12 inhibitor** | ADP receptor (P2Y12) | Blocks the OTHER platelet activation pathway |
| **Heparin/LMWH** | Antithrombin III → factor Xa + thrombin | Blocks the coagulation cascade at multiple levels |
| **Beta-blocker** | Beta-1 adrenergic receptors | Reduces myocardial oxygen demand (HR x contractility) |
| **Statin** | HMG-CoA reductase + plaque biology | Stabilizes plaque, reduces inflammation, lowers LDL |

You gave him aspirin. That's one bolt on the door. You left the other four wide open.

## Spatial Logic: Why ST Depressions Point to the Subendocardium

The myocardium has layers. The subendocardium is the innermost layer — the layer closest to the ventricular cavity. During systole, the ventricle contracts. The intramyocardial pressure is highest in the subendocardium. This compresses the subendocardial vessels. Blood flow to the subendocardium happens mostly during diastole, when the ventricle is relaxed.

When coronary perfusion drops — from a partial thrombus or a tight stenosis — the subendocardium is the first layer to suffer. It's the most oxygen-demanding and the worst-perfused layer. The ischemic subendocardium generates an injury current that points **away** from the overlying electrode. Away = down = ST depression.

Transmural ischemia (STEMI) produces ST elevation because the injury current now involves the full thickness of the wall and points **toward** the electrode. ST depression = subendocardial ischemia. ST elevation = transmural ischemia. Both are acute. Both are ACS. Neither is stable.

The distribution tells you which artery: I, aVL, V2-V6 = anterolateral. That's the LAD territory. The widowmaker. Even though it's only partially occluded right now, this plaque lives in the most dangerous real estate in the heart.

## Contrast: Unstable Angina vs NSTEMI vs STEMI

These are not three different diseases. They are three points on the same spectrum of plaque rupture + thrombosis.

| | Plaque Status | Vessel Occlusion | Myocyte Death | Troponin | EKG |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Unstable Angina** | Fissured cap + non-occlusive thrombus | Partial | None | Negative | ST depression or normal |
| **NSTEMI** | Fissured cap + partially occlusive thrombus | Partial | Yes | Elevated | ST depression or normal |
| **STEMI** | Ruptured cap + fully occlusive thrombus | Complete | Yes — transmural | Elevated | ST elevation |

The management of unstable angina and NSTEMI is **identical**: DAPT + anticoagulation + early invasive strategy. The only difference is the troponin. That's why we call them collectively NSTE-ACS.

Calling this "vasospastic angina" was incorrect. Vasospastic (Prinzmetal) angina is ST **elevation** during pain, occurs at rest, and is caused by coronary spasm — not plaque rupture. This patient has multiple CAD risk factors, ST **depressions**, and recurrent exertional pain. This is plaque rupture. This is unstable angina.

## Connecting Thread: The Untreated Plaque

He knew he had hypertension. He knew he had high cholesterol. A doctor told him. He didn't start the medications.

Three major risk factors — hypertension, hyperlipidemia, smoking — have been silently damaging his coronary endothelium for years. Hypertension creates shear stress on the vessel wall. Hyperlipidemia provides the substrate for foam cell formation. Smoking oxidizes LDL, making it more atherogenic, and directly damages endothelial cells.

The plaque that fissured today was built over decades. The medications he never took would have slowed that process. The smoking he never quit accelerated it. The chest pain he felt today was the first warning. For many patients, the first warning is also the last.

When the case ends, you don't stop caring. Smoking cessation counseling. Low-salt diet. Exercise. Cardiac rehab. Tdap and shingles vaccines. ACE inhibitor for his untreated hypertension. Statin for his untreated hyperlipidemia. Metformin if his HbA1c confirms diabetes. This is the long game — the difference between seeing this patient again in six months with a STEMI versus seeing him in clinic, exercising, off cigarettes, controlled BP, on appropriate meds. 0% on preventive care means you left him without a long game.

## Clinical Anchors

**DAPT is a two-drug regimen for a reason.** Platelets have multiple activation pathways. Blocking one (aspirin/COX-1) leaves the others (ADP/P2Y12, thrombin/PAR-1) wide open. Two is the minimum.

**Anticoagulation adds a second layer.** Antiplatelets block platelets. Anticoagulation blocks the coagulation cascade (thrombin, factor Xa). These are separate systems. Both are activated in ACS. Both need to be suppressed.

**TIMI score changes management.** Score 0-2: conservative (medical management, stress test). Score 3+: early invasive (cath within 24 hours). TIMI 4 means the patient should be in the cath lab, not waiting in the ED.

**Troponin negativity does not equal safety.** A negative troponin at hour 0 means no myocyte death has occurred *yet.* It does not mean no myocyte death *will* occur. The thrombus is still there. The plaque is still unstable. The clock is still ticking.

**Cardiology consult is the door, not the destination.** The cardiologist will "see the patient and give recommendations. Please order any urgent interventions." That means: order the catheterization. The consult doesn't do it for you.

**When the patient says the pain persists, believe them.** At 22:00 — six hours into the case — the patient reports that the chest pain remains, "someone is standing on his chest." The patient is not improving with aspirin and nitroglycerin alone. This is evidence of ongoing ischemia. This is the signal to escalate — transfer to ICU, start the full ACS bundle, get him to cath lab.
