# Ulcerative Colitis — Attending Walkthrough

**Date:** 2026-07-23 | **Score:** 84.9% (Avg 81.7%) | **Patient:** 31-year-old male, business executive

---

## Hook

He's 31. One month of crampy abdominal pain. Four loose stools a day. Blood in the toilet. Weight loss. Reduced appetite. His child is 5. He works an executive job. He's been telling himself it's stress.

The rectal exam shows brown stool tinged with blood, guaiac positive. This is not hemorrhoids. This is not stress. The blood is coming from inside the colon. And it's been doing it for a month.

---

## Mechanism: Mucosal Inflammation — Shallow but Continuous

Ulcerative colitis is a mucosal disease. The inflammation stays in the mucosa and submucosa. It never goes transmural. That's why UC doesn't cause fistulas or strictures — the muscular wall is intact.

But what UC lacks in depth, it makes up for in continuity. The inflammation starts at the rectum and marches proximally. No skip lesions. No normal patches in between. The entire affected segment is uniformly inflamed, friable, and bleeding.

The mucosa is the colon's absorptive surface. When it's inflamed, the colon can't absorb water. The stool is watery. When the mucosa is friable — so fragile that the scope touching it causes bleeding — every bowel movement scrapes open the surface. Blood mixes with stool. The patient sees it in the toilet.

This is the cardinal symptom of UC: **bloody diarrhea.** Not occult blood. Not melena. Fresh blood mixed with loose stool, multiple times a day.

---

## Spatial Logic: The Rectal Gradient

UC always starts at the rectum. Always. That's the definition — if the rectum is spared, it's not UC. It's Crohn's or something else.

The inflammation extends proximally to varying degrees:
- **Proctitis:** rectum only. Bloody stools, tenesmus, urgency.
- **Left-sided colitis:** up to the splenic flexure.
- **Pancolitis:** the entire colon.

This patient's symptoms — 4 BMs/day, blood, weight loss — suggest left-sided or extensive colitis. The colonoscopy would show continuous inflammation from the rectum upward, with loss of vascular pattern, erythema, friability, and exudate.

---

## Contrast: UC vs Crohn's — Quick Bedside Rule

| | UC | Crohn's |
|---|---|---|
| **Rectum** | Always involved | Often spared |
| **Pattern** | Continuous | Skip lesions |
| **Depth** | Mucosal only | Transmural |
| **Bloody diarrhea** | Hallmark | Less common |
| **Perianal disease** | Never | Skin tags, fistulas, abscesses |
| **Strictures/fistulas** | Rare (only in severe chronic) | Common |
| **Smoking** | Protective | Worsens disease |

The rectal exam is the fastest discriminator. If you see perianal skin tags, it's Crohn's. If the stool is frankly bloody with continuous inflammation from the rectum up, it's UC.

---

## Connecting Thread: Why Inflammation Causes Weight Loss

The inflamed colon doesn't absorb nutrients. The patient doesn't want to eat — abdominal pain after meals teaches him to avoid food. Chronic inflammation raises metabolic demand. Cytokines (TNF-alpha, IL-6) promote catabolism. The result: weight loss, muscle wasting, fatigue.

This is not depression. This is not "stress at work." This is a systemic inflammatory disease presenting with GI symptoms. The weight loss should be the clue that this is not IBS. IBS doesn't cause weight loss. IBD does.

## Clinical Anchors

**Stool studies FIRST.** You must rule out infection before diagnosing IBD. C. difficile, bacterial culture (Salmonella, Shigella, Campylobacter, E. coli O157:H7), ova and parasites. If any of these are positive, you treat the infection. If all are negative — and the history is chronic (>4 weeks) with blood and weight loss — you scope.

**Colonoscopy with biopsy** is diagnostic. Continuous inflammation from rectum proximally. Mucosal friability. Loss of vascular pattern. Biopsy shows crypt architectural distortion, crypt abscesses, basal plasmacytosis.

**Mesalamine (5-ASA) is first-line for mild-moderate UC.** It's a topical anti-inflammatory delivered directly to the colonic mucosa. It works because UC is mucosal. In Crohn's, mesalamine has limited evidence because the inflammation is transmural — the drug can't reach the deep layers.

**Corticosteroids (prednisolone) for acute flares.** Rapid symptom control. Taper as soon as possible. Steroids are a bridge, not a destination. Long-term management is mesalamine or biologics.

**Smoking is paradoxically protective in UC.** The mechanism is unclear — possibly nicotine's effect on colonic mucus production or immune modulation. But tell him to quit anyway. Lung cancer does not care about UC epidemiology.
