# Ruptured Ovarian Cyst with Hemoperitoneum — Attending Walkthrough

**Date:** 2026-07-23 | **Score:** 76.4% (Avg 86.9%) | **Patient:** 26-year-old woman

---

## Hook

She's 26. She's tachycardic at 120. Her blood pressure is soft and getting softer. She's clutching her right shoulder while guarding her pelvis. That right shoulder pain is not musculoskeletal. It's blood. Free intraperitoneal blood pooling under the right hemidiaphragm, firing the phrenic nerve, and the brain — confused about where the signal came from — maps it to the C3-C5 dermatome: the shoulder.

This is Kehr sign. When you see it in a reproductive-age woman with acute pelvic pain, you have two diagnoses racing each other: ruptured ectopic and ruptured ovarian cyst. Both kill her the same way. The β-hCG tells you which scalpel to use.

---

## Mechanism: Why a Cyst Ruptures

Every menstrual cycle produces a corpus luteum after ovulation. That structure is a temporary endocrine organ. It's supposed to be richly vascularized — it's pumping out progesterone to maintain a potential pregnancy. The vessel walls are thin, newly formed, and fragile.

Sometimes — no clear trigger, though anticoagulation and trauma raise the odds — one of those vessels tears. Blood spills directly into the peritoneal cavity. There's no tamponade here. The peritoneum is an open space. She bleeds until the pressure drops enough to slow the flow, or until you stop it.

The right ovary ruptures more often than the left. Nobody knows exactly why — anatomy, angulation, differential blood supply — but it means right-sided pain in a woman with free fluid can fool you into thinking appendix.

---

## Spatial Logic: The Shoulder Signal

The phrenic nerve (C3-C5) innervates the diaphragm. The diaphragm shares cervical root origins with the shoulder dermatomes. When blood pools beneath the right hemidiaphragm — the most dependent portion when she's supine — the blood physically irritates the phrenic nerve endings.

The brain receives a signal from C3-C5. It doesn't know the signal originated in the diaphragm. It assumes it came from the skin — because that's what C3-C5 normally reports. So she feels it in her right shoulder.

This is not "referred pain" in the vague sense. This is a wiring error — the diaphragm has no dedicated cortical representation, so the brain defaults to the dermatome map. Gas under the diaphragm does the same thing after laparoscopic surgery. Blood does it faster and with more consequence.

---

## Connecting Thread: The β-hCG Fork

β-hCG is not a pregnancy test here. It's a diagnostic traffic controller.

**Positive β-hCG + empty uterus + free fluid + adnexal mass = ruptured ectopic.** The surgeon does a salpingectomy or salpingostomy.

**Negative β-hCG + empty uterus + free fluid + collapsed ovarian cyst on US = ruptured corpus luteum cyst.** The surgeon does ovarian repair and suction.

Same presentation. Same hemodynamic urgency. Same OR. Different organ. The β-hCG is the only variable that changes the surgical plan. That's why the attending orders it reflexively — not because she "might be pregnant," but because the diagnosis cannot be made without it.

---

## Clinical Anchors

**Never NSAIDs in active hemorrhage.** Ketorolac is an antiplatelet agent. She's already losing blood into her belly. Don't turn off her platelets too. IV fentanyl, hydromorphone, or morphine only.

**Type and screen before she crashes.** She goes to the OR with blood available, or she codes on the table while the blood bank crossmatches. This is not optional.

**Most ovarian cyst ruptures don't need surgery.** Observation, analgesia, and serial exams suffice when the patient is hemodynamically stable and the hemoperitoneum is small. This patient was unstable — large free fluid, dropping hematocrit, hypotension. She met surgical criteria. That's why she went to the OR. Don't let the case fool you into thinking every ruptured cyst needs the knife.

**Pre-op bundle:** 2 large-bore IVs, type and screen, EKG, NPO, consents. Every bleeding surgical patient.
