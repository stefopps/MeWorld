## Mechanism

### Osteoarthritis: Loss of the shock absorber

Picture the knee joint. Two surfaces of articular cartilage — smooth, glassy, frictionless — separated by synovial fluid. The cartilage is a sponge made of collagen type II mesh filled with aggrecan (a proteoglycan). Aggrecan is a bottle-brush molecule: a protein core with thousands of chondroitin sulfate and keratan sulfate side chains bristling out. These side chains are highly negatively charged. They attract sodium. Sodium attracts water. Water pressurizes the cartilage. When you stand, the pressurized water resists compression like a hydraulic cushion.

Osteoarthritis is the failure of this system.

**Step 1: Aggrecan loss.** Age, mechanical stress, and low-grade inflammation activate matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and aggrecanases (ADAMTS-4, ADAMTS-5). They cleave aggrecan. The negative charge density drops. The osmotic swelling pressure collapses. The sponge can no longer hold water.

**Step 2: Collagen network failure.** Collagen type II fibers, normally under tension from the swollen aggrecan, go slack. They fray. Microfibrillation. The surface of the cartilage, once mirror-smooth, becomes rough and fissured.

**Step 3: Subchondral bone reaction.** Without the cartilage cushion, load transfers directly to the subchondral bone. The bone responds by thickening (subchondral sclerosis). Osteophytes form at the joint margins — the body's misguided attempt to stabilize the joint by widening the surface area. These are the "bone spurs" you see on X-ray.

**Step 4: Synovitis.** Cartilage breakdown products (fibronectin fragments, collagen fragments) are pro-inflammatory. They activate synovial macrophages. The synovium becomes inflamed. This adds a inflammatory component on top of the mechanical problem — which is why intra-articular steroids help (they quiet the synovitis, not fix the cartilage).

### Why NSAIDs kill this patient

Peptic ulcer disease means the gastric mucosal barrier is already compromised. NSAIDs make it worse through two mechanisms:

1. **Direct topical irritation.** NSAIDs are weak acids. In the acidic stomach, they're uncharged and cross the epithelial membrane. Inside the cell (pH 7.0), they ionize and are trapped. This "ion trapping" concentrates NSAIDs in gastric epithelial cells, causing direct cytotoxicity.

2. **COX-1 inhibition.** Prostaglandins (PGE2, PGI2) produced by COX-1 maintain the gastric mucosal barrier: they stimulate mucus and bicarbonate secretion, maintain mucosal blood flow, and promote epithelial restitution. NSAIDs block COX-1. Mucus production drops. Bicarbonate secretion drops. Blood flow drops. The stomach lining, already damaged by PUD, loses its last defenses.

The result: a patient with PUD who takes ketorolac has a >10-fold increased risk of GI bleed. The CCS algorithm knows this. The case summary states it explicitly: "NSAIDs should be avoided in this patient as she has a history of peptic ulcer disease, and she states in the HPI that she has adverse reactions to NSAIDs."

### The treatment you should have given

**Duloxetine (SNRI).** Serotonin and norepinephrine are neurotransmitters in the descending pain-modulating pathways from the brainstem to the dorsal horn of the spinal cord. These pathways use 5-HT and NE to inhibit pain transmission at the spinal level. Duloxetine blocks reuptake of both serotonin and norepinephrine, increasing their concentration in the synaptic cleft in these descending pathways. More 5-HT and NE in the dorsal horn = more inhibition of the ascending pain signal from the arthritic knee. This is central pain modulation — not anti-inflammatory, not COX-dependent, completely safe with PUD.

**Intra-articular steroids.** Triamcinolone or methylprednisolone injected directly into the knee joint space. They suppress synovial macrophage activation, reduce MMP and cytokine production, and quiet the inflammatory component of OA. Local injection means minimal systemic absorption — safe with PUD. NOT prednisone (requires hepatic conversion to prednisolone). NOT hydrocortisone (too short-acting). Triamcinolone and methylprednisolone give weeks to months of relief.

**Physical therapy.** Strengthens the quadriceps and hamstrings. A strong quadriceps absorbs some of the load that would otherwise go through the joint. Gait training, assistive devices. Evidence-based first-line treatment.

**Weight loss.** Every pound of body weight translates to 3-4 pounds of force through the knee during walking. Losing 10 pounds removes 30-40 pounds of force per step. This slows cartilage loss.

**Why duloxetine works in OA:** The descending pain-modulating pathway (periaqueductal gray → rostral ventromedial medulla → dorsal horn) uses serotonin and norepinephrine to gate pain signals at the spinal level. Duloxetine increases both. It doesn't fix the knee. It turns down the volume on the pain signal reaching the brain.

Quadriceps strengthening. Gait training. Range of motion exercises. Assistive device assessment. Conservative treatment that modifies disease progression. 0% because you jumped to knee replacement instead.

## What You Over-Ordered (Invasive — Appropriate Orders: 0%)

### Ketorolac — CONTRAINDICATED

The patient told you she has PUD. She told you NSAIDs make her sick. You ordered ketorolac anyway. This is a direct contraindication. The CCS didn't just not give you credit — it flagged this as an inappropriate order. Ketorolac is a potent non-selective COX inhibitor with a GI bleed risk that's lethal in a PUD patient.

### Total Knee Replacement — INVASIVE, TOO EARLY

You jumped from diagnosis to surgery with zero conservative therapy. The OA treatment ladder is: weight loss + PT → duloxetine → intra-articular steroids → THEN consider surgery if refractory. You skipped every rung and went straight to the top. CCS flagged this as invasive.

### Arthrocentesis — INVASIVE, NOT INDICATED

Joint tap for what? The X-ray showed OA. There were no signs of infection (no fever, normal WBC, no erythema/warmth). Arthrocentesis is indicated for suspected septic arthritis or crystal arthropathy — not for radiographic OA. CCS flagged this as invasive.

### STI Testing (Chlamydia, Gonorrhea, Syphilis) — NOT INDICATED

A 60-something woman with chronic knee pain and OA on X-ray. Reactive arthritis (from chlamydia/gonorrhea) presents as acute mono/oligoarthritis in a younger sexually active population, not chronic degenerative joint disease in an older adult. These tests had zero diagnostic yield. They didn't hurt the score, but they added unnecessary orders.

### Stress Test — NOT INDICATED

Knee pain. Not chest pain. Not dyspnea. No pre-op risk stratification needed yet because you shouldn't be operating yet. Not the right test for this case.

## Clinical Pattern

This case exposes a specific blind spot: **medication contraindications.** You've missed this before — ACE inhibitors in renal artery stenosis, aspirin in active bleeding, and now NSAIDs in PUD. The patient tells you what she can't take. You acknowledge it. Then you order it anyway because ketorolac is muscle memory for pain.

**The fix:** When a patient says "I can't take X drug because of Y condition," write it down. Then when you reach for the standard pain/inflammation order, check that note. "Pain → ketorolac" is reflexive. Add a step: "Pain → check contraindications → THEN order."

**The second pattern:** Jumping to invasive treatment before conservative. Same as the brain tumor case (surgery before oncology). Same as this case (knee replacement before duloxetine, PT, and steroid injection). Conservative first. Invasive only when conservative fails.
