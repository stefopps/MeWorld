# Acute Appendicitis in Pregnancy — Attending Walkthrough

**Date:** 2026-07-23 | **Score:** 76.0% (Avg 76.9%) | **Patient:** 28F, 32 weeks pregnant

---

## Hook

She's 28, 32 weeks pregnant, with RLQ pain and rebound. Low-grade fever. Leukocytosis with a left shift. Her OB history is normal. The baby is fine on Doppler.

The uterus is pushing everything cephalad. The appendix is not where you learned it in anatomy lab. In the third trimester, the gravid uterus displaces the cecum and appendix upward and laterally — toward the right upper quadrant. McBurney's point is still a landmark, but by 32 weeks it's not at McBurney's point anymore. The pain can be anywhere from the RLQ to the RUQ to the flank. Do not be fooled by the location.

---

## Mechanism: Why Pregnancy Changes Everything

The growing uterus is a physical force. It starts in the pelvis. By 32 weeks, the fundus reaches the xiphoid. Everything in its path gets compressed and displaced.

The appendix, normally hanging off the cecum in the RLQ, gets pushed superiorly and laterally. The omentum — which would normally wall off an inflamed appendix — gets stretched thin and displaced upward. It can't reach the appendix to contain the infection. So appendicitis in pregnancy perforates faster and spreads more diffusely than in a non-pregnant patient.

At the same time, the physiologic leukocytosis of pregnancy (up to 15,000) masks the WBC elevation you'd normally rely on. The nausea and vomiting are already baseline pregnancy symptoms. The diagnosis hides behind normal pregnancy physiology.

---

## Contrast: Imaging in Pregnancy

CT is contraindicated if you can avoid it. Ionizing radiation to a 32-week fetus is not zero-risk. Ultrasound is first-line — but it's less sensitive than CT, especially in the third trimester when the gravid uterus obscures the appendix.

The graded compression ultrasound technique works: you press the probe where it hurts and look for a non-compressible, blind-ending tubular structure greater than 6 mm in diameter with absent peristalsis. In this patient, that's exactly what they found: 9 mm dilated appendix, echogenic inflammation, no flow on Doppler.

MRI without contrast is the backup when US is equivocal. But if US clearly shows appendicitis — as it did here — you go to OR.

---

## Connecting Thread: Safety in Pairs

Any pregnant surgical patient needs two consultants: general surgery for the appendix, and obstetrics for the fetus. Perioperative fetal monitoring. Tocolysis if needed. Delivery planning if things go wrong.

**NSAIDs are absolutely contraindicated in the third trimester.** They cause premature closure of the ductus arteriosus and oligohydramnios. The ductus arteriosus depends on prostaglandins to stay open in utero. NSAIDs block prostaglandin synthesis. Close the ductus before birth, and the newborn has persistent pulmonary hypertension.

IV opioids — fentanyl, hydromorphone, morphine — are safe in pregnancy for acute pain. Acetaminophen is safe but insufficient for surgical-level pain.

**Ondansetron** is pregnancy-safe and treats the nausea. Don't let her vomit with an inflamed appendix — every retch raises intra-abdominal pressure on a structure that's about to perforate.
