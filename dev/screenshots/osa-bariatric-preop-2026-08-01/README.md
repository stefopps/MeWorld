## Mechanism: Soft Tissue Closes the Pipe

Picture the upper airway as a floppy hose held open by muscle tone while awake.

At sleep onset, pharyngeal dilators quiet. In BMI 43, fat pads in the lateral pharyngeal walls and tongue base already narrow the lumen. When tone drops, the walls approximate. Airflow stops. Oxygen falls. CO2 rises. The brain briefly arouses, tone returns, the airway pops open with a snort, and sleep fragments. Repeat hundreds of times. Morning arrives after nine hours in bed with almost no consolidated restorative sleep. That is daytime hypersomnolence with a "normal" sleep duration.

**Spatial logic.** Pathology is *nocturnal* and *oropharyngeal*. Daytime lung and heart exams can be clean. A normal CBC hematocrit does not rule it out. Facial bone films and an ENT shrug do not open a collapsed airway. Mild pulmonary hypertension on echo is a whisper of chronic intermittent hypoxia, not the diagnosis.

**Contrast — central vs obstructive.** Central apnea is the brainstem forgetting to breathe. Obstructive apnea is the pipe collapsing while the drive to breathe is still there. Obesity points hard at obstruction. Polysomnography separates them.

**Connecting thread — bariatric clearance.** Anesthesia relaxes the same dilators. Untreated OSA means a difficult airway, postop desaturation, and cardiopulmonary risk. The case ends when you order CPAP after a positive sleep study. Insulin and a calorie-restricted diet matter for surgical readiness, but they are not the OSA treatment.

## Clinical Anchors

**CPAP/BPAP is the disease-modifying order.** Positive pressure stents the pharynx. Blood pressure may improve after CPAP; do not chase antihypertensive changes before that follow-up.
