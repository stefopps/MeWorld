# Elder Abuse — Attending Walkthrough

**Date:** 2026-07-28 | **Score:** 56.06% (Avg 84.6%) | **Patient:** 85F, dementia, pain in side after a fall, son controls the interview

## Hook

An 85-year-old woman with dementia falls. Her son brings her in, answers every question, tells you she tripped. But she has contusions on her neck and shoulders — not her side. She has burn wounds. She has both healing and acute rib fractures on imaging. She cannot speak for herself.

You did the right thing consulting social services. But you never imaged her chest. You never gave her pain medication. And you kept her in the office instead of admitting her to the hospital — where the abuser cannot reach her.

This case is not about making the diagnosis. You made it. It's about what happens after.

## Mechanism: The Three Signatures of Abuse

### 1. The Distribution Pattern — Why the Neck, Not the Side

A patient who falls onto their side — laterally, as the son described — should have contusions over the lateral thorax, the iliac crest, possibly the greater trochanter. Those are the impact points in a lateral fall. The physics of the fall dictates the distribution: lateral point of contact → lateral bruising.

This patient had contusions on the neck, shoulders, and upper back. Those are NOT lateral impact points. Those are proximal, axial, central. Those are areas that are grabbed, struck, or restrained — not fallen upon. The distribution pattern refutes the mechanism. A grab-mark on the upper arm or neck is centripetal force applied by another person; a fall is centrifugal force applied by gravity. The direction of the bruises tells you the direction of the force. The force came from the center outward, not from the side inward.

**Burn wounds** in an elderly demented patient follow the same logic. Cigarette burns and scald burns cluster on the upper back and shoulders — areas the patient cannot see or protect. They are inflicted from behind and above, where the caregiver has access and the patient has no defense.

This is the same pattern recognition you use for child abuse: bruises on the ears, neck, torso, and buttocks are abuse until proven otherwise. Bruises on the shins and elbows are play. In elders, bruises on the neck and upper back are abuse until proven otherwise. Bruises on the lateral hip are falls. The distribution is the diagnosis.

### 2. The Radiographic Timeline — Old + New Fractures

One fall produces one set of fractures. The fracture lines are the same age. They have the same radiographic appearance: acute, non-displaced, with soft tissue swelling.

On this patient's chest imaging — which you did not order — there would be:

- **Acute fractures:** sharp cortical breaks, minimal or no callus, adjacent soft tissue swelling, point tenderness
- **Healing fractures:** visible periosteal reaction, bridging callus, rounded fracture edges, no soft tissue swelling

The temporal gap between these fracture sets refutes a single-event mechanism. The patient has been injured before, the body has mounted a healing response, and now she has been injured again. The skeleton is a history book: each fracture line is dated by its callus stage. A rib series with fractures of different ages is the imaging signature of repeated trauma. It is the objective evidence that corroborates the abuse diagnosis and will stand up in court.

Why this matters for CCS: the "chest X-ray" order includes PA, lateral, and rib views. It costs nothing extra to get the rib series at the same time. You get the acute fractures for the acute complaint AND the old fractures for the abuse diagnosis in one order.

### 3. The Narrated History — When the Caregiver Speaks

Dementia patients cannot give a reliable history. They rely on their caregiver to narrate the events. When the caregiver is the abuser, the narrative is weaponized: "She tripped and fell." This is plausible on its face — elderly people fall. But the injury pattern contradicts the mechanism. The caregiver is shaping the presentation to deflect suspicion.

In the CCS case, the scorer explicitly notes that **the son answers all the questions**. This is a planted clue. It means: do not trust the history. Examine the patient yourself. Separate the patient from the caregiver when you do the physical exam. If you see contusions in areas inconsistent with the reported mechanism, the mechanism is false. The history is the abuser's alibi.

## What You Got Right

You consulted social services. That is the single most important order in this case — it scores the majority of your treatment points and is the intervention that protects the patient. You examined the skin and documented the contusions and burns. You examined the chest and lungs. You did not order any inappropriate invasive procedures. Your timing and sequencing were correct. You recognized abuse.

The problem was what you did NOT do after recognition.

## What You Missed

| Missed | Impact |
|--------|--------|
| **Chest imaging (CXR PA/Lateral or CT chest)** | The chief complaint was pain in the side after a fall. You cannot diagnose rib fractures without imaging. More critically: the old-healing + new-acute fracture pattern on the same film is the radiographic proof of abuse. Without imaging, you have a suspicious physical exam but no objective evidence. This is what dropped your diagnostic score to 26.92%. |
| **CBC with differential** | An 85-year-old trauma patient with tachycardia (HR 120) and hypotension (80/45) needs a CBC. You need to know if she is hemorrhaging, anemic at baseline, or mounting a stress response. The leukocyte differential also screens for infection — another common cause of decline in demented patients who cannot report symptoms. |
| **BMP / CMP / Liver function panel** | Frail elderly patients have narrow homeostatic margins. Electrolytes shift fast. Renal function determines what medications she can receive. Liver enzymes screen for hepatic injury from blunt trauma and for chronic alcohol use (another abuse risk factor in caregivers). |
| **Pain medication (NSAIDs or acetaminophen)** | Multiple broken ribs hurt. Uncontrolled pain → splinting → shallow breathing → atelectasis → pneumonia → respiratory failure. In an 85-year-old with dementia, this cascade is not theoretical — it is a near-certainty. The mechanism of death from rib fractures in the elderly is not the fracture itself; it is the pulmonary complication from untreated pain. NSAIDs and acetaminophen are first-line because opioids cause sedation and respiratory depression in frail patients — the exact thing you are trying to prevent. |
| **Correct location — ED or Inpatient Unit** | You kept the patient in the Office for the entire case. An abused elderly patient cannot be discharged to the home of the abuser. She needs admission for: (1) protection from further abuse, (2) pain management for multiple rib fractures, (3) workup for other injuries, (4) social services coordination for safe discharge. The office is not equipped for any of these. You lost half the location score because the patient spent the case in the same room as the person who hurt her. |

## The Closing Framework

When an elderly demented patient presents with trauma and a caregiver who speaks for them:

1. **Examine the patient alone** — separate from the caregiver. The skin exam is the diagnostic test.
2. **Image the injury** — the radiographic pattern (old + new fractures) is your objective proof.
3. **Admit to the hospital** — abuse patients cannot go home. The hospital is the safe zone.
4. **Involve social services** — this is mandatory reporting territory. You got this one right.
5. **Treat the pain** — untreated pain from rib fractures kills elderly patients through hypoventilation and pneumonia. NSAIDs and acetaminophen, not opioids.
