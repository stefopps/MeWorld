The **fever, lethargy, hypotension, and bandemia** signal **overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (OPSI)** , a septic syndrome that can kill within hours.

The opponent is **Streptococcus pneumoniae**. Its polysaccharide capsule evades opsonization and splenic clearance. In an asplenic host, the organism multiplies unchecked, triggering widespread **capillary leak** (hypotension), **cytokine storm** (fever, lethargy), and **consumptive coagulopathy** (not yet evident but imminent). The **WBC 18,000 with 15% bands** reflects a marrow pushed to release immature neutrophils in response to overwhelming bacterial load. The **hemoglobin 8.7 g/dL** is her baseline anemia from sickle disease, but the acute infection accelerates hemolysis and suppresses erythropoiesis.

**Streptococcus pneumoniae** is the most common cause of OPSI in children with sickle cell disease. **Haemophilus influenzae type b** is less common due to vaccination, **Staphylococcus aureus** typically causes focal infections like osteomyelitis, **Klebsiella** is a nosocomial pathogen, and **Neisseria meningitidis** is possible but less frequent in this age group. The mechanism is clear: the absent spleen cannot trap the capsule, so the organism disseminates.

## Concept 6 — A 7-year-old boy is presented at the pediatric clinic with 12 days of persistent cough, nasal stuffiness, purulent nasal discharge, and low-grade fever

This child has **acute bacterial sinusitis** following a viral upper respiratory infection. The **12-day duration** and **purulent nasal discharge** with low-grade fever are the stakes: they signal a secondary bacterial infection, not a self-limited viral process. The opponent is the **mucociliary clearance** mechanism, which is paralyzed by viral inflammation, allowing bacteria trapped in the sinus ostia to proliferate.

**Streptococcus pneumoniae** is the most common cause in this age group. The physiology: viral rhinitis causes mucosal edema that obstructs the **maxillary sinus ostia**, creating a closed space with low oxygen tension. **S. pneumoniae** thrives in this environment because it is a **facultative anaerobe** that can ferment glucose without oxygen, producing lactic acid that further impairs neutrophil function. The thick green mucus reflects **neutrophil degranulation** and **myeloperoxidase activity** from the host response to the polysaccharide capsule of pneumococcus.

Other organisms are less likely. **Non-typeable H. influenzae** is more common in younger children or those with recurrent infections. **Moraxella catarrhalis** produces beta-lactamase but is less frequent in this age. **Anaerobes** require a dental source or longer duration. **Pseudomonas** is seen in immunocompromised or cystic fibrosis patients, not a previously healthy 7-year-old.

## Concept 7 — A 16-month-old boy is brought to the clinic by his parent due to persistent weight loss and frequent diarrhea

This child’s body is **destroying its own intestinal lining** in response to gluten. The **autoimmune attack** targets the small bowel villi, flattening them and eliminating the surface area needed for absorption. The **pale appearance and low hemoglobin with low MCV** signal iron-deficiency anemia: iron absorption occurs in the proximal duodenum, the exact site of maximal villous destruction. The **blistered, symmetric rash on elbows and knees** is dermatitis herpetiformis, the cutaneous expression of the same IgA-mediated immune response attacking the skin’s dermal papillae.

The opponent is **gluten**, specifically the gliadin fraction in wheat, barley, and rye. The **persistent weight loss** reflects caloric malabsorption despite adequate intake. The **tiredness** stems from anemia and generalized nutrient deficiency. No parasites found rules out giardiasis. The age, chronic course, and multisystem involvement -- gut, blood, skin -- point to one mechanism: **autoimmune enteropathy driven by gluten exposure**.

## Concept 8 — A 3-year-old girl presents with a history of persistent ocular discomfort and not tracking objects with her eyes

The opponent here is the **rubella virus** crossing the placenta during the first trimester, when organogenesis is most vulnerable. This virus does not kill cells outright but slows their division, creating a constellation of defects from a single insult. The **persistent ocular discomfort** and failure to track objects stem from **cataracts** and **microphthalmia** -- the virus directly infects the lens, halting its growth and inducing opacification. This creates turbulent flow audible as a bruit. The **thrombocytopenia** and **purpuric lesions** are a direct consequence of the virus infecting **megakaryocytes** in the bone marrow, impairing platelet production. The rash is not an allergic reaction but **extramedullary hematopoiesis** in the skin, where the dermis attempts to compensate for the failing marrow. Each finding -- ocular, auditory, vascular, and hematologic -- shares the same mechanism: rubella’s **direct cytopathic effect** on rapidly dividing fetal tissues.

# Set 70 — Cardiology: Valvular & Structural Heart Disease

## Concept 1 — A previously healthy 38-year-old female, who recently relocated from Mexico, presents with a 3-month history of progressive breathlessness upon walking, without chest pain, dizziness, or fainting spells

This patient’s progressive breathlessness, leg swelling, neck vein distention, S3 gallop, and cardiomegaly point to a **dilated cardiomyopathy** with **biventricular failure**. The key historical clue is the prior surgery for an “enlarged esophagus.” That surgery was likely a **Heller myotomy** for **achalasia**, a condition strongly linked to **Chagas disease** (caused by *Trypanosoma cruzi*). The protozoal infection silently invades cardiac myocytes, triggering a chronic inflammatory and fibrotic process that destroys contractile tissue.

The opponent is **Trypanosoma cruzi** itself. The parasite’s intracellular persistence in the myocardium provokes a **T-cell mediated immune response** that kills infected cells and replaces them with non-contractile scar. This **myocardial fibrosis** progressively weakens the left ventricle’s ability to eject blood, raising **end-diastolic pressure**. That elevated pressure transmits backward, distending the neck veins and driving **transudation** into dependent tissues (pedal edema). The S3 gallop is the sound of blood abruptly decelerating against a stiff, volume-overloaded ventricle during early diastole.

The chest x-ray shows **cardiomegaly** because the failing ventricle dilates to maintain stroke volume via the **Frank-Starling mechanism**, but this compensation eventually fails as the scarred wall cannot generate adequate tension. No murmurs are heard because the valves are structurally normal; the pathology is in the **myocardium**, not the endocardium. The history of achalasia (esophageal dilation) is a classic regional clue: Chagas disease damages the **enteric nervous system** (causing achalasia) and the **cardiac conduction system and myocytes** simultaneously. The other options -- Lyme, hypothyroidism, hypertension, sarcoidosis -- do not produce this specific dual-organ pattern.

## Concept 2 — A 35-year-old female, with a history of untreated hypertension, presents to the clinic with sudden onset of severe headache, nausea, and visual disturbances over the past three weeks

The stakes here are end-organ damage from a pressure surge that overwhelms autoregulation. **Retinal hemorrhages and exudates** are the direct ophthalmoscopic signature of **fibrinoid necrosis** in arterioles, the hallmark pathology of accelerated hypertension. This is not just high pressure; it is pressure that exceeds the vessel wall’s capacity to constrict, forcing plasma and red cells through damaged endothelium into the retina.

**Blood pressure >210/130 mmHg** alone is the trigger, not the diagnostic finding. Many patients tolerate such levels without acute vascular injury if autoregulation holds. **Chest pain** suggests coronary ischemia but is nonspecific. **Heart failure signs** reflect chronic pressure overload, not the acute necrotizing arteriolitis. **Proteinuria** indicates glomerular injury, but it can occur in chronic hypertension or other nephropathies.

The opponent is **mechanical wall stress** exceeding the **myogenic reflex** in small arterioles. When pressure forces dilation despite smooth muscle contraction, the vessel wall undergoes **fibrinoid necrosis**  --  deposition of fibrin into the media. The retina is the only accessible window to this process. **Retinal hemorrhages** (flame or blot) and **soft exudates** (cotton-wool spots from nerve fiber layer infarction) confirm that the pressure has broken through autoregulatory defenses in the brain’s microvasculature, which mirrors the same process in kidney, heart, and brain. This finding demands immediate blood pressure reduction to prevent irreversible cerebral or renal damage.

## Concept 3 — A 78-year-old man living in a long-term care facility presents with fever and a productive cough for the past three days

This patient’s **community-acquired pneumonia** is most likely caused by **Streptococcus pneumoniae**. The stakes are high: in an elderly man with **COPD** and **diabetes**, pneumococcal pneumonia can rapidly progress to respiratory failure or bacteremic sepsis. The **left lower lobe infiltrate** on chest X-ray is a classic radiographic pattern for S. pneumoniae, which triggers a **neutrophil-dominant inflammatory response** that fills alveoli with purulent exudate, producing **rales** on auscultation. The **productive cough** and **fever** reflect this localized infection.

The opponent is **impaired mucociliary clearance** from COPD, which allows aspirated bacteria to colonize the lower airways. S. pneumoniae is the most common cause of **typical pneumonia** in this demographic, outpacing Klebsiella (which favors alcoholics or diabetics with higher mortality) and Pseudomonas (reserved for bronchiectasis or prior antibiotic exposure). The **acute onset** and **lobar consolidation** align with Streptococcus, not the atypical pathogens Legionella or Chlamydia, which cause patchy infiltrates and systemic symptoms like hyponatremia or dry cough.

## Concept 4 — A 50-year-old Asian woman presents to the clinic with a fever, persistent dry cough, and increasing breathlessness over the last day

This patient is fighting **Pneumocystis jiroveci pneumonia**, driven by the physics of an opportunistic pathogen exploiting a specific immune defect. The opponent is her **recently completed lymphoma treatment**, which likely included corticosteroids or chemotherapeutics that depleted her **CD4+ T-cell count**. This cell-mediated immunity gap is the precise niche for PJP, a fungus that cannot survive in an immunocompetent host.

The **bilateral ground-glass opacities** on CT are not random inflammation. PJP adheres to alveolar epithelium, triggering a **proteinaceous exudate** that fills the airspaces. This exudate creates a **diffusion barrier**  --  oxygen cannot cross from alveolus to capillary. That explains the **84% SpO2** and **tachypnea of 30**: the body is racing minute ventilation to compensate for a fixed gas-exchange defect. The **dry cough** arises from airway irritation without significant sputum production, as PJP does not cause a neutrophilic bronchitis.

The **fever of 39.2°C** and **bilateral crepitations** complete the picture: systemic inflammation from the organism, and crackles from fluid-filled, recruitable alveoli. This is not SARS-CoV-2 (no typical exposure, post-chemotherapy timing favors PJP), not candidiasis (systemic Candida requires bloodstream invasion), not CMV (typically retinitis or colitis in this setting), and not chemotherapy pneumonitis (which is subacute and afebrile). The **acute onset with high fever** after immunosuppression is the signature of PJP.

## Concept 5 — A 72-year-old woman with a history of hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, and a 40-pack-year smoking history undergoes elective endovascular aneurysm repair for an infrarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm

This patient’s presentation is driven by **cholesterol embolism syndrome**, a shower of **cholesterol crystals** from the **aortic atherosclerotic plaque** dislodged during the endovascular aneurysm repair. The **opponent** is the mechanical disruption of the plaque’s fibrous cap, releasing crystalline emboli into the downstream circulation.

The **crampy abdominal pain and bloody stools** represent **mesenteric ischemia** from cholesterol crystals lodging in the **superior or inferior mesenteric artery** branches. The **mottled purplish discoloration** on the soles (livedo reticularis and “blue toe” syndrome) is **cutaneous cholesterol embolization**  --  crystals occlude small arterioles, causing inflammation and tissue ischemia. The **elevated creatinine (2.8 mg/dL)** is **acute kidney injury** from emboli to the renal microvasculature, while **eosinophilia** reflects the **inflammatory response** to the foreign cholesterol crystals. The **yellow plaques in retinal arterioles** (Hollenhorst plaques) are **cholesterol emboli** visualized directly in the fundus  --  a pathognomonic finding.

This is a **single mechanism**  --  **atheromatous embolization**  --  expressing in **multiple vascular beds** (gut, skin, kidney, eye) weeks after the inciting event. The **timing** (3 weeks post-procedure) and **normal coagulation labs** (not thrombophilia) rule out acute arterial thrombosis or antiphospholipid syndrome. Systemic vasculitis and endocarditis lack this **crystalline embolic pattern** and the **retinal plaques**.

## Concept 6 — A 35-year-old elementary school teacher presents to the emergency department with a 2-week history of worsening shortness of breath, especially when lying flat, and swelling in his legs

The bedside stakes are high: this patient has **acute decompensated heart failure** with a **dilated cardiomyopathy** and an **ejection fraction of 30%**. The opponent is a **viral infection** that directly invades and injures cardiac myocytes, most commonly **coxsackievirus B** or **echovirus**.

The mechanism begins with viral entry into cardiomyocytes, often via the **coxsackie-adenovirus receptor**. Viral replication triggers a **direct cytotoxic effect**, causing myocyte necrosis and release of intracellular contents. This is followed by a **host immune response** that, while attempting to clear the virus, also causes **collateral damage** through cytotoxic T cells and cytokine release. The result is **global hypokinesis** and **left ventricular dilation** as the weakened myocardium fails to generate adequate systolic force. The **S3 gallop** is the sound of blood abruptly decelerating against a noncompliant, volume-overloaded ventricle. The **bibasilar crackles** and **jugular venous distension** are hydrostatic consequences of elevated left ventricular filling pressure pushing fluid into the lung interstitium and backward into the systemic venous system.

The **recent fever and cough** one month prior is the sentinel event: a **prodromal viral illness** that precedes cardiac symptoms by days to weeks. The **nonspecific ST-T changes** on ECG reflect diffuse myocardial inflammation without a coronary distribution. The **young age** and **absence of coronary risk factors** make ischemic cardiomyopathy unlikely. **Hypothyroidism** causes a bradycardic, low-output state, not this high-output failure pattern. **Autoimmune myocarditis** is possible but far less common than viral. **Coccidioidomycosis** is a fungal infection restricted to endemic regions and typically presents with respiratory symptoms, not acute heart failure.

# Set 88 — Renal & GU: Kidney Disease & Electrolytes

## Concept 1 — A 28-year-old man who is incarcerated presents with a 4-week history of low-grade fever, productive cough with occasional hemoptysis, and sharp right-sided chest pain that worsens with deep breathing

This patient’s **4-week subacute course** with **hemoptysis**, **pleuritic chest pain**, **weight loss**, and **night sweats** points to a slowly destructive lung process, not an acute bacterial infection. The **failed 7-day levofloxacin** trial is the critical bedside clue: community-acquired pneumonia pathogens are susceptible to fluoroquinolones, so no response means the opponent is **Mycobacterium tuberculosis**, which replicates slowly and lives inside macrophages, not in the airway where levofloxacin works.

The **diminished breath sounds over the right upper lung** localize the pathology to the apical-posterior segments. Tuberculosis thrives in areas of high oxygen tension -- the **upper lobes** -- because the organism is an obligate aerobe. The **hemoptysis** arises from caseous necrosis eroding into bronchial arteries, while **pleuritic pain** indicates inflammation extending to the parietal pleura. The **sputum Gram stain showing no organisms** is expected: mycobacteria require **acid-fast staining** due to their mycolic acid cell wall, which also makes them resistant to standard antibiotics.

The **poorly controlled diabetes** is a key host factor: hyperglycemia impairs macrophage phagolysosome fusion and T-cell recruitment, allowing mycobacterial replication unchecked. The **incarceration** adds epidemiological risk. Sarcoidosis typically has bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy and non-caseating granulomas, not cavitary upper lobe disease with hemoptysis. Influenza pneumonia is acute, not 4-week, and lacks the weight loss and pleuritic pattern.

## Concept 2 — A 58-year-old woman with a history of long-term smoking presents to the clinic with a two-month history of painless gross hematuria, unintentional weight loss of 10 pounds, and persistent low-grade fever

The patient’s **painless hematuria**, **constitutional symptoms**, and **right kidney mass with local invasion** point to **renal cell carcinoma** as the most likely diagnosis. ## Concept 3 — A 62-year-old man with a history of type 2 diabetes mellitus and hypertension develops end-stage renal disease and begins hemodialysis three times per week

The most common cause of death in this patient is **cardiovascular disease**, accounting for over 70% of mortality in dialysis patients. The opponent here is **accelerated atherosclerosis** driven by the unique metabolic environment of end-stage renal disease. Uremic toxins promote **endothelial dysfunction** and a **pro-inflammatory state**, while **volume overload** from intermittent hemodialysis creates recurrent left ventricular strain. This patient’s **uncontrolled hypertension** and **smoking** compound the injury, forcing the heart to work against a rising afterload.

The mechanism is a vicious cycle: **hyperphosphatemia** from failed renal excretion triggers **vascular calcification**, stiffening arteries and raising pulse pressure. Each dialysis session removes fluid, but the rapid shift in intravascular volume can cause **myocardial stunning** and **arrhythmias**. Over months to years, this process produces **left ventricular hypertrophy**, **coronary ischemia**, and **sudden cardiac death**.

At the bedside, this means that managing the patient’s **blood pressure**, **phosphate levels**, and **smoking cessation** directly targets the root cause of mortality. The high prevalence of cardiovascular death dwarfs risks from infection, stroke, or dialysis withdrawal, making it the primary focus for improving long-term survival.

## Concept 4 — A 42-year-old man presents to the clinic with complaints of frequent urination, especially at night, and occasional dull pain in his lower back

This patient is losing kidney function through **cystic replacement of parenchyma**, and the diagnosis is **Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease (ADPKD)**. The stakes are progressive renal failure, hypertension, and risk of cerebral aneurysm. The opponent is the **PKD1 or PKD2 gene mutation** that disrupts primary cilia function in tubular epithelial cells, causing unchecked tubular dilation and fluid accumulation.

The **bilateral flank tenderness** and **numerous renal cysts on ultrasound** are the structural signature. Cysts compress adjacent nephrons, causing **ischemic atrophy** and **interstitial fibrosis**. This reduces glomerular filtration rate, reflected in the **serum creatinine of 2.1 mg/dL** and **BUN of 35 mg/dL**. The **nocturia** occurs because damaged tubules lose concentrating ability, forcing high urine output overnight. The **microscopic hematuria** (5-10 RBCs/HPF) comes from cyst rupture or bleeding into the urinary tract. The **enlarged nodular liver** is a key extrarenal expression: **hepatic cysts** arise from the same ciliary defect in biliary epithelium, and they are present in over 80% of ADPKD patients by age 60.

The **family history** is the epidemiologic hammer. His mother’s kidney transplant and brother’s CKD at relatively young ages point to a **dominant inheritance pattern** with high penetrance. His **hypertension** is both a consequence and a driver: cyst expansion activates the **renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system** by compressing renal arterioles, worsening fibrosis and accelerating GFR decline. This is not chronic pyelonephritis (no bacteria, no WBC casts, no scarring pattern), not Alport syndrome (no hearing loss, no characteristic glomerular basement membrane thinning), and not diabetic nephropathy (normal glucose, no proteinuria).

## Concept 5 — A 72-year-old retired mechanic with a 50-pack-year smoking history presents to the clinic after noticing painless blood in his urine over the past two weeks

The stakes here are high: **painless hematuria** in a **72-year-old with a 50-pack-year smoking history** is **bladder cancer** until proven otherwise. The mechanism is straightforward. Cigarette smoke delivers carcinogens like **beta-naphthylamine** and **4-aminobiphenyl** into the bloodstream. These are filtered by the kidneys, concentrated in the urine, and then stored in the bladder. Prolonged contact with the **transitional epithelium** of the bladder wall drives **DNA adduct formation** and **p53 tumor suppressor mutations**, leading to **urothelial carcinoma**. The tumor grows as a **friable, exophytic mass** that bleeds easily into the urine without causing pain, because it does not obstruct flow or inflame the bladder wall.

The urinalysis confirms the pattern: **>50 RBCs/HPF** with **no WBCs**, **negative leukocyte esterase**, **negative nitrites**, and **no bacteria**. This is **sterile hematuria**  --  blood without infection. The opponent is **time and tumor invasion**. Benign prostatic hyperplasia causes hematuria from friable prostate vessels, but it is typically intermittent and often accompanied by obstructive voiding symptoms, not painless gross hematuria. IgA nephropathy presents with **sympharyngitic hematuria** (blood with an upper respiratory infection) and often proteinuria. Nephrolithiasis causes **colicky flank pain** with microscopic or gross hematuria. Bacterial cystitis would show **pyuria**, **positive leukocyte esterase**, and **bacteriuria** on culture. The absence of all these forces points directly to a **bladder tumor** eroding into a vessel.

## Concept 6 — A 42-year-old man presents to the clinic for evaluation of hypertension discovered during a routine health screening

The bedside stakes here are progressive kidney failure. The opponent is a genetic structural defect that forces nephron destruction through relentless cyst expansion. The **dull bilateral flank pain worsened by standing** is caused by enlarging cysts stretching the renal capsule. # Set 116 — MSK & Ortho: Trauma, Fracture, Joint Disorders

## Concept 1 — A 16-year-old girl is brought to her family physician by her father due to concerns about her recent erratic behavior

The clinical stakes here are prognostic and therapeutic: this patient’s **four-month duration** of psychotic symptoms sits at the diagnostic boundary between a disorder that often remits fully and one that implies lifelong impairment. The opponent is **time itself**. Schizophrenia requires **six months** of continuous signs. Schizophreniform disorder captures the window from **one to six months** with the same core psychotic features  --  delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech, negative symptoms  --  but without the chronicity that defines schizophrenia.

The mechanism of her symptoms lies in **dopaminergic dysregulation** within the mesolimbic pathway. Excessive dopamine activity in the ventral striatum and nucleus accumbens produces **positive symptoms**: the **mythical creatures** are complex auditory hallucinations (thought waves), and the **guiding life choices** represent a **delusional system** of reference. The **disconnected phrases** (alogia) and **social withdrawal** reflect **negative symptoms** from reduced dopaminergic tone in the prefrontal cortex  --  the same dopamine system, different regional expression. **Avoiding eye contact** is a behavioral manifestation of social withdrawal, not a separate finding.

The **four-month duration** is the decisive differentiator. Schizoaffective disorder would require a concurrent major mood episode (depression or mania) for a substantial portion of the illness, which is absent here. Brief psychotic disorder lasts **less than one month**. Major depressive disorder with psychotic features would have mood-congruent delusions and a prominent depressive syndrome preceding or dominating the psychosis. This patient’s **pure psychotic syndrome** at **four months** fits **schizophreniform disorder**  --  a provisional diagnosis that predicts a **50% chance of full recovery** versus progression to schizophrenia.

## Concept 2 — A 29-year-old Latina woman is brought to the clinic by her partner after noticing she suddenly stopped using her left hand three days ago

The key bedside stake is distinguishing **organic paralysis** from **functional neurological symptom disorder** because the latter carries no risk of stroke or structural lesion, yet demands specific therapeutic engagement. The patient’s **la belle indifférence**  --  calmness in the face of sudden left-hand paralysis  --  is a classic but not pathognomonic feature. The critical mechanism lies in **intact deep tendon reflexes** and **normal sensation**, which together rule out an upper motor neuron lesion (which would hyperreflexia) and a lower motor neuron or sensory tract injury. The **ice pack test** is the decisive opponent: holding cold over the left wrist temporarily restored voluntary movement. This works because cold stimulation activates **A-delta fibers** (fast pain and temperature), which bypass the patient’s suppressed voluntary motor pathways by recruiting **subcortical motor circuits** that are not under conscious control. In organic paralysis, cold would not restore movement because the structural damage blocks all motor output. The intact reflexes and sensation confirm the motor cortex and corticospinal tract are structurally normal; the deficit is a **functional disconnection** between intention and execution, often driven by psychological stress or trauma. This pattern fits **functional neurological symptom disorder** (conversion disorder), not malingering (which lacks la belle indifférence and would not respond to ice), factitious disorder (where the patient consciously produces symptoms for a sick role), or somatic symptom disorder (where distress dominates over motor loss).

## Concept 3 — A 6-year-old girl was rushed to the hospital following a fall from a swing, landing on her outstretched right hand

The stakes here are **tissue viability** in the limb. The opponent is **ischemia from rising intracompartmental pressure**. A **supracondylar fracture** in a child, from a fall on an outstretched hand, often injures the **brachial artery** at the elbow. Bleeding and edema inside the tight fascial compartments of the forearm cannot expand. As pressure exceeds **capillary perfusion pressure (~30 mmHg)**, venous outflow collapses first, then arteriolar inflow stops. The result is a **compartment syndrome**, which causes pain **disproportionate to the injury** and unrelieved by analgesics.

The **warm fingertips and brisk capillary refill** are a dangerous distraction. They reflect **arterial inflow above the compartment**, not perfusion of the deep muscles and nerves under pressure. Pain with **passive stretch of the wrist flexors** is the earliest and most reliable sign, preceding pulselessness by hours. Once pulses vanish, **muscle necrosis and Volkmann contracture** are already established. **Nerve injury** (choice B) and **vascular spasm** (choice C) can coexist but do not explain the escalating pain despite adequate analgesia. **Fracture angulation** (choice D) is a radiographic concern, not an acute pain crisis. **Psychogenic pain** (choice E) is implausible with a clear mechanical trigger and objective swelling. The priority is **urgent fasciotomy** to decompress the compartments before irreversible damage occurs.

## Concept 4 — A 45-year-old Asian woman experiences a jolt of intense low back pain after stepping onto an uneven sidewalk

The patient’s **chronic stiffening of the spine over 25 years** points to **ankylosing spondylitis**, a seronegative spondyloarthropathy. This disease progressively **ossifies the spinal ligaments and facet joints**, fusing vertebrae into a rigid column. A fused spine loses its normal shock-absorbing flexibility. The **jolt from stepping onto uneven sidewalk** transmits force directly to the vertebral bodies without the damping effect of mobile segments. That concentrated axial loading **overwhelms the bone’s structural integrity**, producing a **compression fracture**  --  often a wedge fracture of a single vertebra.

The acute pain is focal, mechanical, and triggered by a specific low-energy event. The **lumbar tenderness** localizes to the fracture site. This is not an inflammatory flare, which would be more diffuse, involve morning stiffness, and respond to NSAIDs  --  which she already uses regularly. **Lumbosacral radiculopathy** would radiate pain below the knee with dermatomal sensory changes, absent here. **Referred pain** from abdominal pathology lacks the mechanical trigger. **Fibromyalgia** presents with widespread pain and tender points, not a jolt-induced focal back pain.

The opponent is **bone fragility from spinal fusion**: the rigid spine cannot distribute load, so a trivial force fractures the vertebra. The **stakes** are high  --  an unrecognized fracture can cause progressive kyphosis, neurologic compromise, or pseudarthrosis. Management requires **bed rest, bracing, and possibly kyphoplasty**, not just NSAIDs.

## Concept 5 — A 14-year-old girl is brought to the pediatrician by her foster parents due to ongoing behavioral issues over the past year

The key distinction here is the **pattern of antagonistic behavior** directed specifically at **authority figures**, not peers or siblings. This is the hallmark of oppositional defiant disorder. The opponent is the child’s **dysregulated emotional and behavioral response to perceived control**, driven by a **low frustration tolerance** and a **negative affectivity** that primes her to interpret neutral or corrective interactions as hostile. The behavior is **reactive and oppositional** -- arguing, defying, blaming others -- without the **violation of others’ rights** or **aggression** that defines conduct disorder.

In oppositional defiant disorder, the **core deficit** is in **emotion regulation** and **social information processing**. The child’s brain over-attends to **threat cues** from authority, triggering a **fight response** (arguing, vindictiveness) rather than a **flexible adaptation**. This is **not** normal adolescent behavior because it is **persistent** (over a year), **pervasive across multiple settings** (school, home, extracurriculars), and causes **functional impairment** (poor grades, conflict with teachers). Major depressive disorder is ruled out by the **absence of anhedonia, sleep/appetite changes, or pervasive sadness** -- irritability alone is not sufficient. Conduct disorder requires **aggression toward people or animals, property destruction, theft, or serious rule violations**, none of which are present. ADHD typically presents with **inattention, impulsivity, or hyperactivity** that is **situationally inconsistent** and not primarily oppositional toward authority.

## Concept 6 — A 42-year-old man presents to the clinic with a 3-week history of progressive pain in his left lower leg

The patient’s pain is **localized point tenderness** over the **posteromedial tibial border**, worsened by **repetitive weight-bearing** and improved by **rest**. This is a **stress fracture** until proven otherwise. The opponent is **repetitive mechanical load exceeding bone remodeling capacity**. The tibia, as a weight-bearing long bone, experiences **compressive and tensile forces** during running. When osteoclastic resorption outpaces osteoblastic deposition from insufficient recovery, **microcracks propagate** through cortical bone. The **periosteum** is richly innervated, so focal inflammation from these microcracks produces **sharp, localized pain** at the stress point.

A **normal plain radiograph** does not exclude a stress fracture. Radiographs are only **10% sensitive** in the first 2-3 weeks because cortical disruption must reach **30-50% bone loss** to appear as a lucent line or periosteal reaction. The **natural history** is that continued loading without rest converts a **low-risk incomplete fracture** into a **complete displaced fracture**, which requires surgical fixation.

Medial tibial stress syndrome presents with **diffuse pain** over the distal posteromedial tibia, not a discrete point. Chronic exertional compartment syndrome causes **cramping, tightness, and paresthesias** that resolve only after cessation of activity, not with rest alone. Deep vein thrombosis would show **warmth, edema, and a positive Homan’s sign**, not focal bone tenderness. Osteomyelitis requires **fever, erythema, and elevated inflammatory markers**, none of which are present.

## Concept 7 — A 35-year-old woman presents with a 2-week history of poorly localized pain on the lateral aspect of her right knee

The most likely diagnosis is **Iliotibial band syndrome**. The opponent here is **repetitive friction** between the iliotibial band and the **lateral femoral epicondyle** during knee flexion-extension cycles. The **snapping sensation** is the IT band sliding over the epicondyle, and the **pain at 0-30 degrees of flexion** is the zone where the IT band impinges most tightly against the bone. **High-intensity interval training** with squats, lunges, and hill sprints drives this repetitive microtrauma.

The **tenderness proximal to the lateral joint line** localizes to the IT band insertion, not the **lateral collateral ligament** which lies more posterior and would hurt with varus stress. **No joint effusion or instability** rules out a **lateral meniscus tear** or ligament injury. **Patellofemoral pain syndrome** causes anterior knee pain, not lateral snapping. **Pes anserine bursitis** is medial, not lateral. The **pressure over the lateral epicondyle during active flexion** reproduces the exact mechanical conflict, confirming the diagnosis.

# Set 91 — Nutrition & Metabolism: Vitamins, Diet, Cachexia

## Concept 1 — A 74-year-old woman presents to her primary care physician for a routine follow-up visit

This patient is losing a fight against **secondary hyperparathyroidism** driven by **vitamin D deficiency** and **inadequate calcium intake**. Her 25-hydroxyvitamin D of **14 ng/mL** is severely deficient. Low vitamin D reduces intestinal calcium absorption. The resulting drop in ionized calcium triggers the parathyroid gland to release **PTH 112 pg/mL**. PTH pulls calcium from bone to normalize serum levels, but at the cost of **accelerated bone resorption**. Her **T-score of -2.7** confirms osteoporosis. The **alkaline phosphatase 142 U/L** reflects high bone turnover from that PTH-driven osteoclast activity.

Her **calcium 8.6 mg/dL** must be corrected for **albumin 3.3 g/dL**. Corrected calcium = 8.6 + (4.0 - 3.3) × 0.8 = **9.16 mg/dL**  --  low-normal, not adequate for bone mineralization. Hydrochlorothiazide reduces urinary calcium excretion, but that cannot compensate for absent intake. Her diet is protein-poor and calcium-poor. She has **sarcopenia** (BMI 21.8, visible muscle wasting, slow gait), which independently raises fall and fracture risk.

The correct combination addresses every link in the chain. **Calcium carbonate 1,200 mg daily** provides the substrate PTH is scavenging from bone. **Vitamin D3 800–1,000 IU daily** restores absorption and suppresses PTH. **High-protein dietary counseling** reverses sarcopenia and improves muscle strength. **Pharmacologic osteoporosis therapy** (bisphosphonate or anabolic agent) directly inhibits osteoclasts or builds bone  --  necessary because her T-score is below **-2.5** and she already fractured. Options B through E each miss one or more essential components: B ignores calcium needs and sarcopenia; C is insufficient for severe deficiency; D does not address bone density or nutrition; E would worsen sarcopenia and does not treat the underlying deficiency.

## Concept 2 — A 58-year-old South Asian woman with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes presents for dietary counseling

The patient is losing a fight against **glycemic load** -- the total carbohydrate burden that her insulin-deficient state cannot handle. Her **HbA1c of 8.6%** means her average glucose is ~200 mg/dL, driven by the **rapidly absorbed starch** in white rice and chapati, plus the **simple sugars** in sweetened chai. The group class handout created a **cultural chasm** -- it banned staple foods without offering substitutions, triggering a sense of erasure. That makes adherence impossible. The correct response (C) uses **collaborative modification**, not elimination. You keep the **glycemic index** lower by reducing rice portion (e.g., half the usual), retaining **lentils** -- their fiber and protein blunt postprandial glucose spikes -- and switching to **unsweetened chai** to cut ~10-15 g of sugar per serving. Reducing **ghee** addresses the **saturated fat** that worsens insulin resistance over months, not hours. This approach respects **cultural identity** as a **social determinant of adherence** -- a patient who feels heard is far more likely to make small, sustainable changes. The other options fail because they impose **binary rules** (A, B, D) or a **prescriptive template** (E) that ignores her cooking reality. The **opponent** here is not her culture -- it's the **glycemic load** of her traditional portions. Meet her where she cooks.

## Concept 3 — A 44-year-old man with hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and a BMI of 33 kg/m² presents for a routine follow-up

The patient's **LDL of 146 mg/dL**, **blood pressure of 144/88 mmHg**, and **fasting glucose of 104 mg/dL** are three expressions of a single underlying process: **dietary excess of saturated fat and sodium** driving **atherogenic dyslipidemia**, **volume-mediated hypertension**, and **insulin resistance**. Red and processed meats are dense sources of **saturated fat** (raising LDL by downregulating hepatic LDL receptors) and **sodium** (expanding plasma volume and increasing afterload). Processed meats also contain **N-nitroso compounds** that directly damage colonic epithelium, initiating carcinogenesis. Reducing these foods removes the opponent -- **excess saturated fat and sodium** -- allowing **LDL clearance**, **natriuresis**, and **reduced colonic mutagen exposure**.

The environmental impact is a **parallel convergent mechanism**: livestock production, especially ruminants, generates **methane** (a potent greenhouse gas) and requires **vast land and water** per gram of protein. Reducing red and processed meat consumption simultaneously **lowers greenhouse gas emissions**, **frees agricultural land**, and **reduces water consumption**. The patient’s motivation is clinically useful -- he can leverage one behavioral change (reducing red/processed meat) to achieve **both lipid lowering and blood pressure improvement** while also **lowering colorectal cancer risk** and **reducing his dietary carbon footprint**. This is not an all-or-nothing proposition; **partial reduction** yields proportional benefits in both domains.

## Concept 4 — A 54-year-old woman with primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) managed with ursodeoxycholic acid presents for an annual review

This patient’s **chronic cholestasis** is the core problem. Primary biliary cholangitis destroys small intrahepatic bile ducts, reducing bile salt secretion into the duodenum. Without sufficient intraluminal bile salts, the **critical micellar concentration** cannot be reached. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) require micelle incorporation for absorption. The lab values confirm this: low **25-hydroxyvitamin D 9 ng/mL**, low **vitamin A**, low **vitamin E**, and elevated **INR 1.4** from vitamin K deficiency.

The most clinically significant consequence is her **severe osteoporosis** (T-score -3.1). Low vitamin D → reduced intestinal calcium absorption → **hypocalcemia 8.2 mg/dL** → secondary **hyperparathyroidism (PTH 148 pg/mL)**. Elevated PTH accelerates bone resorption, worsening the kyphosis and antalgic gait. This is a **fracture risk emergency**. Ursodeoxycholic acid improves cholestasis but does not fully restore micelle formation; she still needs supplementation. The xanthelasma and xanthomas reflect **hyperlipidemia from impaired bile acid synthesis**, not an urgent priority.

The opponent is **bile salt deficiency** fighting the patient’s ability to absorb fat-soluble vitamins. The consequence is **bone loss from vitamin D malabsorption** driving secondary hyperparathyroidism.

## Concept 5 — A 67-year-old woman with stage G4 CKD (eGFR 24 mL/min/1

CKD reduces **nephron mass**, which directly limits the kidney's capacity to excrete a sodium load. Each remaining nephron can increase fractional excretion, but the absolute ceiling is fixed. Her **24-hour urine sodium of 5,200 mg** proves she is excreting that massive load, but only at the cost of maintaining a high plasma volume. The **7 lb weight gain over four weeks** is the clinical footprint of that retained sodium pulling water into the extracellular space. Volume expansion drives her hypertension and worsens her heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. Loop diuretics like furosemide block the Na-K-2Cl cotransporter in the thick ascending limb, but they cannot overcome a daily sodium intake that exceeds the kidney's maximal excretory rate. Diuretics create a new steady state at a higher volume; they do not restore euvolemia when dietary sodium is extreme.

Her **canned soups, deli turkey sandwiches, soy sauce, and restaurant meals** are the specific sources. Each contains sodium in forms -- sodium chloride, sodium phosphate, monosodium glutamate -- that are fully absorbed and contribute to the same renal sodium burden. The **hyponatremia (134 mEq/L)** is dilutional from water retention driven by ADH release in response to effective volume depletion, not sodium depletion. The **hyperkalemia (5.4 mEq/L)** and **low bicarbonate (19 mEq/L)** reflect the combined effects of CKD, ACE inhibition, and the type IV renal tubular acidosis common in diabetic or hypertensive nephropathy. Sodium restriction is uniquely critical here because the kidney has lost its reserve. No dose of lisinopril or furosemide can bypass the fundamental physics: sodium in minus sodium out equals volume.

## Concept 6 — A palliative care team is consulting on a 78-year-old woman with stage IV gastric cancer who has been declining for six weeks with progressive dysphagia, anorexia, and weight loss of 31 pounds over three months

This is a battle between **the metabolic chaos of cancer cachexia** and the **normal physiology of starvation**. The patient is not starving; she is cachectic. **Cachexia** is driven by tumor-derived cytokines like **TNF-α, IL-6, and proteolysis-inducing factor** that force skeletal muscle and adipose tissue breakdown regardless of caloric intake. **Starvation** is a pure caloric deficit where the body adapts by reducing metabolic rate and sparing protein. **TPN cannot reverse cachexia** because the catabolic signals override any anabolic effect of delivered nutrients.

The family sees **weight loss** and interprets it as starvation. You must explain that **cachexia is a paraneoplastic syndrome**, not a nutritional deficiency. TPN in an **ECOG 4** patient with comfort-focused goals introduces **line sepsis, fluid overload, and electrolyte derangements** -- all sources of suffering. **Oral pleasure feeding** (ice chips, favorite tastes) addresses the emotional need without the physiologic harm. The **MOLST** form is the legal expression of her autonomy; honoring it is not abandonment but fidelity to her values.

The opponent here is **the family’s grief-driven fear of watching her die**. Your job is to redirect their protective instinct toward **comfort measures** -- mouth care, positioning, symptom control -- that actually align with her stated wishes and her body’s irreversible metabolic trajectory.

## Concept 7 — An 81-year-old man is referred to his primary care physician after his daughter notices he has lost approximately 9 kg (20 lb) over the past 4 months without attempting to diet

This patient has **involuntary weight loss >5% of body weight in 6 months** with **early satiety** and **temporal wasting**. The opponent here is **occult malignancy**. His 30 pack-year smoking history, age, and symptom constellation place him in a high-risk group where **cancer is the most common serious cause** of unintentional weight loss. Early satiety suggests a **space-occupying lesion** in the upper abdomen, likely **pancreatic or gastric primary**, compressing the gastric fundus or invading the celiac plexus. Normal basic labs do not rule out malignancy; they simply reflect that **liver synthetic function, kidney function, and hematopoiesis** have not yet been compromised.

The **physics of cancer cachexia** involves **tumor-driven systemic inflammation** -- cytokines like IL-6 and TNF-α suppress appetite, accelerate muscle proteolysis, and increase resting energy expenditure. This is why **nutritional supplements alone fail**; the metabolic set point is shifted against weight gain. **Megestrol acetate** carries a **thromboembolism risk** and only adds fat, not lean mass. **Reassurance** is dangerous because early-stage malignancy is curable. **Dietitian referral** without imaging misses the diagnosis.

The correct next step is **age-appropriate cancer screening** plus **CT chest/abdomen/pelvis** to find the lesion driving the cachexia. This is a **diagnostic urgency**, not a nutritional one.

## Concept 8 — A 41-year-old woman with a known diagnosis of celiac disease, established 5 years ago on the basis of elevated anti-tTG IgA and biopsy-proven villous atrophy, presents for a follow-up visit

This patient has **refractory celiac disease**  --  defined by persistent villous atrophy and symptoms despite ≥12 months of strict gluten avoidance. The negative **anti-tTG IgA** confirms adherence; serology normalizes when gluten is removed, so the ongoing mucosal damage is **gluten-independent**.

The **marked steatorrhea** (positive fecal fat) tells us the small bowel is failing to absorb fat. That requires extensive villous injury  --  not just patchy inflammation. The **increased intraepithelial lymphocytes** on biopsy are the hallmark. In refractory celiac, these lymphocytes can undergo **clonal expansion**  --  a premalignant state called **type II refractory celiac disease**. This carries a 50% risk of progression to **enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma**.

The opponent here is **an aberrant T-cell clone** that has escaped gluten dependence. It drives the same **cytokine-mediated crypt hyperplasia and villous blunting** as active celiac, but without dietary triggers. The next step is **immunophenotyping** of the intraepithelial lymphocytes  --  looking for **loss of surface CD3 and CD8** with **intracellular CD3 positivity**. That distinguishes type I (benign, polyclonal) from type II (clonal, high-risk). Referral to a specialized center is essential for **flow cytometry** and consideration of **immunomodulatory therapy** like **azathioprine** or **cladribine**.

Empiric antibiotics or fiber would miss the diagnosis. Reassurance ignores the lymphoma risk. Restarting gluten is dangerous  --  it would accelerate the clonal process.

## Concept 9 — A 38-year-old woman presents to her primary care physician 18 months after undergoing Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) for morbid obesity

This patient has **vitamin B12 deficiency** after RYGB. The **terminal ileum** is the site of B12 absorption. RYGB bypasses the stomach’s parietal cells and much of the proximal small bowel, but the **critical loss is intrinsic factor** -- produced in the stomach -- which is required for B12 uptake in the ileum. Without intrinsic factor, oral B12 cannot be absorbed. Her **MCV 112 fL** and **hypersegmented neutrophils** confirm megaloblastic anemia. The **decreased vibratory sensation** signals **subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord** -- demyelination of the dorsal columns from B12 deficiency. This is a **neurologic emergency**. Delaying treatment to draw methylmalonic acid or homocysteine risks **irreversible paralysis**.

The opponent here is **progressive demyelination**. B12 is a cofactor for **methionine synthase** and **methylmalonyl-CoA mutase**. Without it, methylmalonic acid accumulates and disrupts myelin synthesis. The **tingling** and **vibratory loss** are early signs; without intervention, spastic ataxia and blindness follow.

**IM vitamin B12** bypasses the absorption defect entirely. **Packed red cell transfusion** is not indicated -- her hemoglobin is 8.4 g/dL, she is stable, and transfusion would suppress her marrow’s own recovery. **Oral folate** would worsen neurologic symptoms by consuming residual B12. **Bone marrow biopsy** is unnecessary when the peripheral smear and labs are diagnostic. The correct step is **initiate intramuscular vitamin B12 injections** and educate on lifelong supplementation.
