## Concept 1 — A 30-year-old male recently joined a new company in a demanding sales role

The stakes here are recognizing that this patient’s **anxiety is pervasive and chronic**, not tied to a single trigger. The **opponent** is a dysregulated **amygdala-hypothalamic-prefrontal circuit** that fails to extinguish worry signals. In Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD), the **basolateral amygdala** becomes hyperexcitable, generating excessive threat detection even in neutral contexts. This drives the **prefrontal cortex** into a loop of compensatory rumination, attempting to “solve” perceived dangers that don’t exist. The result is **persistent muscle tension**, **sleep fragmentation from racing thoughts**, and **irritability** -- the latter due to depleted serotonin reserves in the **raphe nuclei**, which normally buffer emotional reactivity.

The **diagnostic key** is the **6-month duration** of worry about multiple domains (work, relationships, daily tasks) with **poor control** over the worry. Unlike **Adjustment Disorder**, there is no identifiable acute stressor (e.g., job loss). Unlike **Panic Disorder**, there are no discrete panic attacks with autonomic surges. Unlike **OCD**, the thoughts are not intrusive, ego-dystonic obsessions requiring ritualized compulsions -- they are realistic (if exaggerated) concerns about performance and relationships. The **racing thoughts** in GAD are future-oriented “what if” cascades, not the repetitive, unbidden images of OCD. This patient’s **snapping at his partner** reflects the **low frustration tolerance** from chronic hyperarousal, not a compulsion to neutralize a thought.

## Concept 2 — A 27-year-old man comes to the clinic claiming he is unable to resume his normal lifestyle after a traumatic car accident four months ago

This patient’s symptoms are driven by a **failure of fear extinction** in the **amygdala and prefrontal cortex**. Four months after the trauma, the **hippocampus** has encoded the accident as a vivid, fragmented memory. The **amygdala** remains hyperreactive to trauma reminders, triggering a **sympathetic surge** (panic, anxiety) without proper inhibitory input from the **medial prefrontal cortex**. This is not a simple stress reaction; it is a **persistent threat-detection circuit** that cannot distinguish past danger from present safety.

The **nightmares** represent **memory reconsolidation gone awry** -- the brain repeatedly attempts to process the trauma during REM sleep but fails to integrate it, so the emotional charge remains high. The **hypervigilance** to noises is a **sensitized startle reflex** mediated by the **locus coeruleus** and **noradrenergic system**, which primes the body for fight-or-flight at subthreshold stimuli. **Social withdrawal** and **concentration deficits** are downstream effects: the prefrontal cortex is overwhelmed by constant threat monitoring, depleting cognitive resources for focus and social engagement.

The key differentiator is **duration**. Acute stress disorder resolves within one month. At four months, this meets the **PTSD threshold** of symptom persistence beyond 30 days. Adjustment disorder lacks the **intrusive re-experiencing** (nightmares, panic triggers) and the **physiological hyperarousal** that define PTSD. Generalized anxiety disorder has diffuse worry, not trauma-specific triggers. Major depression could co-occur but does not explain the **cue-elicited panic** or **trauma-linked nightmares**. The opponent here is **time and failed neural adaptation** -- the brain’s fear circuitry has become stuck in a maladaptive loop.

## Concept 3 — A 22-year-old college student is evaluated in the emergency department after his roommates report a significant change in his behavior over the past 4 months

The stakes here are diagnostic precision: this patient’s **4-month symptom duration** is the critical boundary separating a disorder with a better prognosis from a lifelong illness. The opponent is **time itself** -- the natural history of psychotic disorders unfolds along a strict timeline, and the diagnosis hinges on where the patient falls on that continuum.

The patient meets full **A-criteria for schizophrenia**: delusions (thought broadcasting, persecution), auditory hallucinations (voices discussing harm), disorganized speech, flat affect, and negative symptoms (isolation, poor grooming). However, the required **6-month duration** for schizophrenia has not yet been reached. At **4 months**, the diagnosis is **schizophreniform disorder**, which by definition lasts between **1 and 6 months** and includes functional decline. The underlying mechanism is the same dopaminergic dysregulation in mesolimbic (positive symptoms) and mesocortical (negative symptoms) pathways, but the temporal cutoff reflects a different natural history -- about **two-thirds of schizophreniform cases** convert to schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder, while the remainder remit.

Schizoaffective disorder is excluded by the **absence of mood episodes** (no depression or mania). Delusional disorder lacks the hallucinations, disorganized speech, and negative symptoms present here. Substance-induced psychosis is ruled out by **no history of substance use**. The 4-month duration places this squarely in schizophreniform territory, making it the most likely diagnosis.

## Concept 4 — A 52-year-old woman with a history of rheumatoid arthritis is admitted for a severe flare and started on high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone

The core issue here is a **medication-induced psychotic disorder** from high-dose glucocorticoids. Corticosteroids like methylprednisolone cross the blood-brain barrier and directly bind to glucocorticoid receptors in the **hippocampus and prefrontal cortex**. This binding dysregulates dopamine signaling, specifically increasing mesolimbic dopamine activity. The opponent is the brain’s normal inhibitory control over dopamine release, which is overwhelmed by steroid-driven glutamatergic excitation. The result is **psychosis with paranoid delusions and auditory hallucinations**, emerging days after high-dose therapy.

The patient’s **full alertness and orientation** rule out delirium, which requires fluctuating consciousness or inattention. Her **normal vitals and labs** exclude infection or metabolic encephalopathy. The **absence of prior psychiatric history** and **acute onset** three days into steroid treatment make schizophrenia or bipolar disorder unlikely. The **denied substance use** eliminates substance-induced psychosis. The **temporal link** to high-dose methylprednisolone is the decisive clue: the drug is the causal force, not a preexisting vulnerability. This is a **dose-dependent** effect, more common with intravenous pulses. Management is to taper the steroid and consider antipsychotics if symptoms persist.

## Concept 5 — A 52-year-old male accountant is brought to the emergency department by his wife due to increasingly erratic behavior over the past month

This patient’s presentation is driven by **sympathetic overdrive** from chronic **methamphetamine toxicity**. The **formication** (tactile hallucinations of insects) is a classic dopaminergic effect: methamphetamine floods the **mesolimbic pathway**, causing sensory misattribution where normal tactile stimuli are interpreted as threats. The **cachexia and dental caries** (meth mouth) result from **vasoconstriction** reducing blood flow to oral mucosa and prolonged **xerostomia** from salivary gland dysfunction, compounded by bruxism and acidic drug byproducts. The **excoriated lesions** on forearms and neck are direct physical evidence of picking at non-existent parasites -- a behavior driven by the same dopamine excess that produces paranoia.

The **paranoid delusions** (hidden cameras, guarding computer) stem from **dopamine D2 receptor hyperactivity** in the prefrontal cortex, impairing reality testing. Unlike schizophrenia, this is acute-on-chronic with clear **sympathetic signs**: weight loss, agitation, insomnia. The **urine toxicology** will confirm amphetamines. Cocaine (choice E) has a shorter half-life and rarely produces this sustained, severe psychosis with physical stigmata. Delusional disorder lacks the cachexia, caries, and excoriations. Schizophrenia typically presents earlier in life without the profound weight loss and dental decay. The opponent here is **chronic catecholamine excess** eroding the patient’s body and mind simultaneously.

## Concept 6 — A 35-year-old woman presents to her primary care physician with complaints of intense anxiety, recurrent nightmares, and difficulty concentrating that began 10 days ago after she narrowly escaped a building collapse during an earthquake

The stakes are distinguishing a **time-limited peritraumatic reaction** from a **chronic condition** that requires different treatment intensity. This patient’s symptoms began **10 days ago** and include intrusion (nightmares), avoidance (news), negative mood (numbness), and hyperarousal (startle, insomnia). The opponent is the **natural recovery window** that follows severe threat exposure.

The diagnosis hinges on **duration**. **Acute stress disorder** is defined by symptom onset **3 days to 1 month** after trauma, with at least **9 symptoms** from five categories (intrusion, negative mood, dissociation, avoidance, arousal). Her symptoms fit this cluster precisely. **Posttraumatic stress disorder** requires symptoms lasting **over 1 month**. **Generalized anxiety disorder** lacks a discrete traumatic trigger and requires **6 months** of worry. **Panic disorder** involves recurrent unexpected panic attacks with fear of future attacks, not tied to a specific event. **Adjustment disorder** involves emotional or behavioral symptoms in response to an identifiable stressor, but it does **not** include the hallmark re-experiencing, avoidance, or hyperarousal of trauma-spectrum disorders.

The **physiology** underlying her symptoms is the **amygdala-hippocampal-prefrontal circuit** dysregulation. The earthquake triggered an **intense sympathetic surge** (norepinephrine, cortisol) that **overconsolidates** the traumatic memory in the amygdala. The **hippocampus** fails to contextualize the event as past, so the **prefrontal cortex** cannot suppress fear responses. This produces **re-experiencing** (nightmares) and **hyperarousal** (startle). The **emotional numbness** reflects **dissociative** coping -- a temporary **dorsal vagal shutdown** to prevent emotional overwhelm. Because this is **acute** (<1 month), the brain has not yet established the **chronic fear extinction deficit** seen in PTSD.

## Concept 7 — A 28-year-old graphic designer presents to her primary care physician complaining of ongoing mood fluctuations that have affected her work and relationships for the past 4 years

The patient’s core problem is a **chronic, low-grade mood instability** that has persisted for **4 years** without ever fully remitting for more than **2 months**. The key finding is **subthreshold hypomanic periods**  --  increased energy, creativity, and decreased sleep without the impairment, grandiosity, or psychosis of full mania. These alternate with **mild depressive episodes** that never meet criteria for major depression (no weight changes, suicidal ideation, or work incapacity). This pattern is the hallmark of **cyclothymic disorder**.

The opponent is a **dysregulated limbic system** that fails to maintain mood homeostasis. In cyclothymia, the **noradrenergic and serotonergic tone** oscillates within a narrower amplitude than in bipolar I or II. The **hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis** generates mild cortisol surges during hypomanic phases, driving the **reduced sleep need** and **goal-directed behavior**. During depressive phases, **dopaminergic signaling** in the prefrontal cortex drops, producing **anhedonia and social withdrawal** but not the profound motor slowing or neurovegetative changes of a major depressive episode.

This case is not **bipolar I disorder** because there are no full manic episodes. It is not **persistent depressive disorder** because the patient has clear hypomanic periods. It is not **borderline personality disorder** because the mood shifts are **episodic and self-limited**, not triggered by interpersonal stressors, and there is no history of identity disturbance or frantic efforts to avoid abandonment. The **chronicity** (4 years) and **subthreshold polarity** force the diagnosis of cyclothymic disorder.

## Concept 8 — A 52-year-old male accountant presents to his primary care physician with concerns about his eating patterns over the past year

The patient’s **loss of control** during eating episodes, consumption of **large quantities** in a discrete period, and **marked distress** afterward point to a disorder of reward circuitry dysregulation, not caloric restriction. The absence of **compensatory behaviors** like purging or fasting is the pivotal distinguishing feature. His **BMI of 28** places him in the overweight range, which is typical for this condition.

The opponent here is the **homeostatic satiety signal** from the gut. In binge eating disorder, the **dopaminergic reward pathway** in the nucleus accumbens overrides the **leptin and cholecystokinin** signals that normally terminate a meal. The patient continues eating past fullness because the **orbitofrontal cortex** fails to inhibit the drive for hedonic reward. Eating in **secrecy** and at **night** reflects the shame-driven avoidance of judgment, which reinforces the cycle through cortisol-mediated cravings.

Unlike bulimia nervosa, there is no **vomiting or laxative abuse** because the **vagal afferent** response to gastric distension does not trigger a compensatory purge; instead, the discomfort is endured. This distinguishes it from **anorexia nervosa** (restriction, low BMI) and **pica** (non-nutritive substances). The diagnosis is **binge eating disorder**, defined by recurrent binges without inappropriate compensatory behaviors.
