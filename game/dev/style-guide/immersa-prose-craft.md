# Immersa Attending — Prose Craft Style Guide

> Internal document. Synthesized from McKee's *Story* and Truby's *The Anatomy of Story*, re-expressed as applied craft technique for the Immersa attending voice. Not a summary of either book — this is a standalone working manual for writing First Principles medical explanations.

---

## 1. The Compression Principle

### Maximum payload per sentence

Every sentence carries exactly one piece of work and no more. The ideal sentence does three things simultaneously: it delivers a fact, advances the causal thread, and creates appetite for the next sentence. If a sentence does only one of these, it is under-loaded. If it does none, it is dead weight.

The test: read the sentence aloud. If you can remove any word without losing meaning, remove it. If you can merge it with the next sentence, merge them. The prose should feel like a tendon under tension — no slack, no decorative fat, no "interestingly" or "notably" or "it is worth noting that."

**The Pascal standard:** A short sentence takes longer to write than a long one because you must decide what to leave out. The compression is in the decisions, not the typing.

### What compression is not

Compression is not brevity for brevity's sake. A sentence stripped to "Glucose is high, insulin is low" has been compressed into meaninglessness — it lost the causal relationship. True compression preserves and intensifies the causal link: fewer words but more connection between them.

## 2. The Gap Engine

### Expectation, then rupture

The basic unit of narrative momentum is not the sentence but the **gap** — the space that opens when a reader expects one thing and gets another. You create movement not by listing facts in sequence but by setting up expectations and then delivering results that are truthful but not obvious.

The simplest form: *"You'd think X, but actually Y."* But the gap is more powerful when it is implicit — when the reader supplies the expectation themselves and the text quietly overturns it.

**Example pattern:**

> *The alveolar membrane is only two cells thick. A single layer of type I pneumocytes on one side, a single endothelial cell on the other, a basement membrane thinner than a wavelength of visible light between them. The distance from air to blood is half a micron. And yet oxygen doesn't just drift across — it needs a pressure gradient, a matching of ventilation to perfusion, a hemoglobin shuttle system that loads and unloads in precise sequence. The thinness creates the possibility; everything else does the work.*

The gap here: "only two cells thick" sets the expectation that gas exchange is trivial; "and yet" opens the gap. The rest of the paragraph closes it.

### The gap at sentence level

Every sentence can contain a micro-gap. The simplest device: a sentence that begins by conceding something intuitive, then pivots with the mechanism.

> *The pancreas senses glucose just fine in type 2 diabetes. The problem isn't detection — it's that the message doesn't land.*

The first sentence establishes the expected (pancreas works), the second opens the gap (insulin resistance downstream). Two short sentences, one gap.

### What kills the gap

- Explaining before you've created the question the explanation answers
- Stating the mechanism before establishing why it matters
- Using "interestingly," "notably," or "it turns out that" as substitutes for actual surprise
- Lists that itemize without building tension between items

## 3. Causal Chaining

### Each sentence is the answer to the previous sentence's question

This is the central discipline. After you write a sentence, the next sentence should feel like the inevitable response to the pressure the previous sentence created. The chain works like this:

1. Sentence A establishes a fact or mechanism.
2. Sentence A creates a question in the reader's mind (often "why?" or "so what?" or "how?").
3. Sentence B answers that question while creating a new one.
4. Sentence C answers B's question.

This is not a formula — it's a diagnostic for your own prose. After writing, read each adjacent pair and ask: "Does the second sentence exist because the first sentence created a need for it, or does it just happen to come next?"

A flat sequence looks like this:

> *The SA node fires at 60–100 bpm. The AV node delays the impulse by 0.1 seconds. The bundle of His conducts it to the ventricles.*

Each sentence is factually correct. None creates pressure that the next resolves. The reader is informed but not pulled forward.

A causal chain:

> *The SA node fires at 60–100 bpm, but that signal can't reach the ventricles directly — the fibrous skeleton of the heart blocks it. So the impulse funnels through a single gate: the AV node, which holds it for 0.1 seconds. That delay isn't waste — it gives the atria time to empty before the ventricles contract.*

Every sentence feels necessary because the one before it created a "why" or "how" it answers.

### The trunk test

If you can rearrange the order of your sentences and the paragraph still "makes sense," the prose is flat. Causal chains are order-dependent — break the chain and the logic breaks. That's the test.

## 4. Stakes Before Mechanism

### Why should I care before how does it work

The reader's attention is drawn by consequence, not by anatomy. Before you explain a mechanism, establish what it costs the patient — what breaks, what worsens, what the finding means at the bedside. The mechanism is the answer to a question the reader now genuinely has.

**Wrong order (mechanism first):**

> *ACE inhibitors block the conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II. Angiotensin II is a potent vasoconstrictor and stimulates aldosterone release. Therefore, blocking it reduces blood pressure.*

This is correct and completely flat. The reader hasn't been told why any of this matters, so the mechanism arrives without context.

**Right order (stakes first):**

> *When the kidneys sense low perfusion, they deploy a chemical cascade that raises blood pressure at a cost — tighter vessels, more sodium retention, more work for a heart that may already be failing. An ACE inhibitor intercepts that cascade at its bottleneck: the enzyme that converts angiotensin I to its active form. Stop that conversion, and you stop the vasoconstriction, the sodium retention, the remodeling.*

The mechanism now feels like the answer to a problem the reader has been shown. The explanation has a reason to exist.

## 5. The Opponent Principle

### Explanation needs resistance

An explanation without opposition is a lecture. An explanation with opposition is a story. For medical prose, the "opponent" is whatever the body is fighting against or whatever makes a clinical decision difficult: a pathogen, a physiological limit, a diagnostic ambiguity, a therapeutic trade-off.

The opponent makes the mechanism feel like work — like something hard won rather than simply stated. It creates the sense that understanding this took effort and that the outcome is not guaranteed.

**Without opponent:**

> *Insulin lowers blood glucose by promoting glucose uptake in muscle and adipose tissue and inhibiting hepatic gluconeogenesis.*

**With opponent:**

> *After a meal, glucose floods the bloodstream faster than cells can absorb it. Insulin is the body's response — it signals muscle and fat to open their glucose channels and tells the liver to stop making more. But in type 2 diabetes, the signal is there and the receptors are there, yet the glucose stays in the blood. The cells have learned to ignore insulin. The hormone is speaking; no one is listening.*

The opponent (insulin resistance) transforms the paragraph from a definition into a problem.

### The necessary opponent

The opponent must be specific to the mechanism being explained. It cannot be a generic "the body struggles" or "nature is complex." It must be a named, concrete force: a receptor that's been downregulated, a pressure gradient that's been reversed, an enzyme that's been inhibited. The reader should be able to point to the opponent in the explanation and say, "That's what makes this hard."

## 6. The Specificity Anchor

### One concrete detail does the work of three abstractions

Abstract medical prose talks about "impaired oxygen delivery." Concrete prose says *"the PaO₂ is 52 — roughly a third of what the mitochondria expect."* The number doesn't just inform; it anchors. It makes the abstract physical.

The rule is: if you can replace a general term with a number, a named structure, or a sensory detail, do it. Not every sentence — that becomes a data dump. But at least once per paragraph, ground the abstraction in something specific.

**Abstract:** *Metformin reduces hepatic glucose output.*

**Anchored:** *Metformin enters the hepatocyte through OCT1 transporters and gently brakes gluconeogenesis — not by blocking it entirely, but by nudging the mitochondrial respiratory chain at complex I, shifting the cell's energy balance so glucose production drops by about a third.*

The specificity isn't decorative; it's what makes the mechanism credible.

## 7. Paragraph Rhythm

### Three to five paragraphs, each doing one job

A First Principles explanation should have the rhythm of a short essay, not a phone book. Each paragraph has a distinct job:

1. **The stakes paragraph:** Why this matters — what's at risk, what goes wrong, what the finding means at the bedside.
2. **The mechanism paragraph:** How it works — the causal chain, the structures, the forces.
3. **The tension paragraph:** What opposes it — the opponent, the trade-off, the diagnostic ambiguity.
4. **The resolution paragraph:** What the intervention does — where it intervenes, what changes, what the outcome is.
5. **The probe paragraph (optional):** A closing question or implication that extends the thinking.

Not every explanation needs all five. Sometimes the mechanism and tension are one paragraph. Sometimes the resolution is the probe. But the rhythm should feel like climbing stairs — each paragraph a step that couldn't have been taken without the one before it.

### Paragraph transitions

The transition between paragraphs lives in the gap — the unanswered question the previous paragraph left hanging. The next paragraph's first sentence should feel like it's picking up a thread the reader already wants pulled. Avoid transitional phrases like "Furthermore," "Additionally," or "Another important point is." If the paragraphs are truly causal, the connection is self-evident.

## 8. Voice and Authority

### The attending doesn't hedge

The Immersa attending speaks with the confidence of someone who has seen this pattern a hundred times. Hedging language — "may be," "it is thought that," "some evidence suggests" — is appropriate when the evidence is genuinely uncertain, but most clinical mechanisms are well-established. State them as fact.

Wrong: *"It is believed that the juxtaglomerular apparatus plays a role in regulating blood pressure."*

Right: *"The juxtaglomerular apparatus is the kidney's pressure sensor. When it detects a drop in perfusion, it releases renin — the first domino in a cascade that ends with angiotensin II clamping down arterioles and aldosterone holding onto sodium."*

### Never write down

The reader is assumed to be intelligent and curious, just not yet expert. Don't simplify the concept — simplify the language around it. The concept should remain rigorous; the sentences around it should remove obstacles to understanding. "Simple words, complex ideas" — not the reverse.

### The bold/blue scan rule

Key anchors — lab values, exam findings, named mechanisms, pivotal structures — should be bolded (they render blue in the UI). A reader scanning only the bolded text should get a skeleton of the explanation: the problem, the mechanism, the intervention. The unbolded text is the connective tissue — the causal chain that makes the skeleton move.

## 9. The Pull Principle

### Questions pull harder than statements push

Facts stated in sequence push the reader through the material. Questions embedded in the prose pull them. The most potent form: a question the reader has already begun asking by the time you articulate it.

Weak: *"Now let's consider what happens when the SA node fails."*

Strong: *"So what fires when the SA node doesn't?"*

The strong version works because the reader was already headed toward this question — the preceding paragraph set it up. The question feels like the reader's own thought, voiced.

Open a paragraph with a question the material has earned. Close a paragraph with an implied question the next paragraph will answer. Questions are the joints between ideas; they're where the writer says, "I know what you're wondering, and here's the answer."

## 10. Compression + Chain: The Integrated Model

When all principles work together, the prose achieves what we can call **loaded flow**: every sentence carries weight (compression), every sentence earns the next (causal chaining), the stakes are visible before the mechanism resolves them (stakes-first), and a specific anchor holds the abstraction to the physical world (specificity).

The anchor sentence from our existing prompt captures this perfectly:

> *"The combination of a big baby and polyhydramnios mechanically overdistends the uterus."*

Why it works:
- It names two causal forces ("big baby" and "polyhydramnios") — specificity.
- It states their combined effect in one clause ("mechanically overdistends") — compression.
- It implies a consequence (the overdistended uterus can't contract effectively — postpartum hemorrhage risk) — causal chain.
- It doesn't waste a word — economy.

This is the standard. Not every sentence can or should hit this density, but every paragraph should have at least one sentence that does.

## Quick Reference Checklist

Before finalizing any First Principles explanation, run this check:

1. **Compression:** Can any word be removed without losing meaning? Are there filler transitions?
2. **Gap:** Does each paragraph create a question that the next answers? Or is the arrangement arbitrary?
3. **Causal chain:** If you read only the first sentence of each paragraph, does it form a coherent argument?
4. **Stakes:** Does the reader know why this matters before learning how it works?
5. **Opponent:** Is there a named, specific resistance force driving the explanation forward?
6. **Specificity:** Is there at least one number, named structure, or sensory detail per paragraph?
7. **Voice:** Is the prose confident? No hedging where the science is settled? Simple words, complex ideas?
8. **Bold scan:** Do the bolded terms alone convey the skeleton of the explanation?
9. **Pull:** Are there questions where the reader is already asking them?
10. **Anchor sentence:** Is there at least one sentence per section that does the work of the model sentence above?

## 11. Structural Variation Forms

Eleven distinct structural templates. The goal is rotation — never use the same form twice in a row. Each form dictates the *opening move* and the *organizing frame*, not the paragraph count or depth. Any form can be used at any depth level; the form controls *how the explanation arrives*, not how long it is.

### Form 1: The Wrong Turn (in medias res correction)

**Opening move:** Voice the plausible wrong answer a tired resident would blurt out, then pivot.

> *The reflex here is to call it heart failure. It's not — and here's why that reflex fails.*

**Structure:** The opening names a specific wrong answer that's *tempting given the presentation* — not a straw man, not an absurd choice, but the answer that would genuinely fool someone who stopped thinking one step early. The rest of the explanation dismantles why that reflex is wrong by revealing the mechanism that contradicts it. Ends when the correct answer becomes inevitable through elimination of the wrong one.

**What makes it distinct:** The wrong answer is stated explicitly and concretely in the first sentence. Not "many would think" or "it's tempting to assume" — the actual diagnosis someone would write in the chart.

### Form 2: The Ticking Clock

**Opening move:** Open with a time-pressure consequence, not the diagnosis.

> *Every hour this goes untreated, the myocardium loses contractile units it will never get back.*

**Structure:** The organizing frame is a clock running. The mechanism is explained as a function of time — what happens at minute 0, what's lost by hour 2, what becomes irreversible by hour 6. The diagnosis is almost secondary; it's the *reason the clock is ticking*. The explanation moves forward in time, not through concepts.

**What makes it distinct:** Time is the spine, not the diagnosis. Numbers attach to minutes or hours, not to lab units alone.

### Form 3: The Lab Value as Plot Twist

**Opening move:** Present a single number cold, no context, then reveal what it eliminates.

> *The sodium is 118. That alone rules out dehydration. It also rules out SIADH from lung cancer — because this number is too low for that mechanism. Here's why.*

**Structure:** The explanation withholds the diagnosis until after the number has done its work. The single value is introduced alone, then used to eliminate 2–3 wrong answers one by one. Only after the field is cleared does the explanation name what remains. The correct answer arrives as the last possibility standing, not the first one stated.

**What makes it distinct:** The lab value opens the explanation, not the clinical presentation. The diagnosis is revealed, not announced.

### Form 4: The Autopsy (backward reconstruction)

**Opening move:** Open at the bad outcome — what happens if this is missed.

> *If you miss this, the patient arrests within 48 hours. Let's rebuild how we got here.*

**Structure:** The end state is stated first — the worst consequence of missing the diagnosis. Then the mechanism unfolds in reverse: from consequence back through decompensation, back to the initial insult. Each step answers "what had to have failed for this to happen?" rather than "what happens next?" The explanation moves backward through the causal chain.

**What makes it distinct:** The arrow of explanation runs consequence → cause, not cause → consequence.

### Form 5: The Overheard Conversation

**Opening move:** Frame as overhearing two clinicians mid-disagreement.

> *One voice in the room is arguing for thrombolytics. Another is pushing back — "look at the CT again." One of them is right, and the reasoning matters more than the answer.*

**Structure:** The explanation unfolds as a back-and-forth between two positions — one arguing for the wrong answer, one countering with mechanism. The reasoning surfaces through the exchange rather than through a single authoritative voice. Ends when the correct position wins on the strength of its mechanism, not when the author summarizes.

**What makes it distinct:** Two positions, each voiced explicitly. The wrong side gets to make its best case before losing. No "on the one hand / on the other hand" academic hedging — this is a real argument between two people who both know medicine.

### Form 6: The Red Herring Tour

**Opening move:** Walk through wrong answers before naming the right one.

> *Three answers on this list would fool a reasonable clinician. Let's walk through each — because the trap in each one teaches more than the right answer alone.*

**Structure:** The wrong answers are listed and dismantled in order of decreasing plausibility. Each gets a specific, mechanistic reason for elimination — never "this is wrong because it doesn't fit." The correct answer arrives last, as the only one that survives the tour. The explanation ends when the last alternative falls, not with a separate conclusion paragraph.

**What makes it distinct:** The correct answer is the *last thing stated*, not the first. The entire explanation is an elimination process.

### Form 7: The Single Sentence Anchor

**Opening move:** One dense sentence containing the entire mechanism, then unpack it.

> *The combination of a big baby and polyhydramnios mechanically overdistends the uterus, and an overdistended uterus cannot contract — that's the entire mechanism of postpartum hemorrhage in this patient. Now let's slow that down.*

**Structure:** The first sentence is the whole explanation compressed. Every clause in that sentence becomes a paragraph: the forces named, the mechanical effect, the clinical consequence. The rest of the explanation is just expanding what was already there. No new concepts are introduced after the opening — only the ones named in the first sentence, unfolded.

**What makes it distinct:** Mechanism-first, not mechanism-last. The entire causal chain is stated in one breath before any elaboration begins. The rest is magnification, not addition.

### Form 8: The Spiral (Truby — returning deeper)

**Opening move:** State a finding at surface level, then announce you're going deeper.

> *On the surface, this looks like volume overload — edema, crackles, JVD. Go one layer deeper and it's not volume at all. Go deeper still and you'll see why the treatment that fixes volume overload will kill this patient.*

**Structure:** The same finding or sign is visited three times, each time at a deeper mechanistic level. First visit: what it looks like clinically. Second visit: what organ system is actually failing. Third visit: what's happening at the cellular or molecular level that makes the first two layers deceptive. Each return reveals something the previous layer actively concealed. The correct answer only becomes clear at the deepest layer.

**What makes it distinct:** The finding doesn't change — the depth of explanation does. Three concentric rings around the same observation.

### Form 9: The Gap Engine (McKee — expectation, then rupture)

**Opening move:** Open with what the learner expects, then overturn it immediately.

> *You'd think a low sodium means too much water. That's what every textbook starts with. But sodium doesn't measure water — sodium measures the kidney's ability to handle water. Those are different things, and confusing them is how you miss the diagnosis.*

**Structure:** Every paragraph follows the same two-beat rhythm: (1) state the expectation — what a reasonable clinician would conclude from this finding alone, (2) rupture it with mechanism — why that conclusion is incomplete or wrong, and what's actually happening. No paragraph is a flat statement of fact. Every paragraph contains a gap between what the surface data says and what the mechanism reveals. The explanation ends when the last expectation has been overturned and only the correct mechanism remains standing.

**What makes it distinct:** Every paragraph is a miniature gap — expectation first, then rupture. The reader is never allowed to settle into a simple fact. No "you'd think X, but actually Y" repetition — each gap must be genuine and specific to that finding.

### Form 10: The Revelation Ladder (Truby — evidence before mechanism)

**Opening move:** Provide the first finding without context. Then the second. Then the third. Only then reveal what they mean together.

> *Finding one: the JVP is elevated. Finding two: the liver edge is pulsatile. Finding three: there's a holosystolic murmur at the left lower sternal border that gets louder with inspiration. Separately, these are three isolated signs. Together, they are one diagnosis — and here's the mechanism that ties all three into a single story.*

**Structure:** Present 3–4 key findings one at a time, stripped of interpretation. Each appears as a standalone observation — no connective tissue, no "this suggests." After all findings are on the table, the mechanism is revealed, and it explains all of them simultaneously. The explanation doesn't start until all the evidence has been displayed. No finding is explained before the next one is revealed; the tension builds through accumulation.

**What makes it distinct:** Mechanism is deferred until after all evidence is visible. The diagnostic reveal is the moment the mechanism is finally stated — it earns its arrival by explaining everything at once.

### Form 11: The Ironic Turn (McKee — the cure IS the diagnosis)

**Opening move:** Name the treatment that should work, explain why it will fail, then reveal that the failure mechanism IS the diagnosis.

> *Lasix should fix this edema. In this patient it won't — the kidneys will pull the sodium back as fast as the loop diuretic kicks it out. And the reason they'll pull it back isn't a Lasix dosing problem. It's the diagnosis.*

**Structure:** Open with the therapeutic instinct — what any reasonable physician would reach for first. Then explain, mechanistically, why that treatment will fail in this specific patient. The failure isn't a footnote — it's the central revelation. The mechanism of treatment failure is identical to the mechanism of disease. Stating why the treatment won't work is stating the diagnosis. The explanation ends when the treatment failure has been fully explained — because at that point, the diagnosis has been fully explained too.

**What makes it distinct:** Treatment failure is the diagnostic key, not a complication. The wrong treatment isn't a mistake — it's the teacher. The irony is structural: trying to fix it reveals what's broken.

### Rotation rule

**Never repeat the previous form.** The same form used twice in a row is a structural failure. Forms 1–7 rotate round-robin; each generation advances the index. The form is a constraint on *how the explanation moves*, not on what it contains — prose craft rules (compression, opponent, bold, stakes) still apply inside whatever form is active.
